Chapter 5 — Economic Growth

Growth, Natural Resources and Climate Change

Land and natural resources in a Solow model

Introduction

Growth with finite resources and climate change

Two constraints enter the growth models:

Exhaustible resources

Production uses a resource whose remaining stock falls with extraction.

Climate change

Production affects the climate; climate damage then affects the economy.

Central question

How do resource depletion and climate change alter economic growth?

Starting point

The Limits to Growth: the Meadows report

The problem

The Meadows report asks whether continuing economic and population growth is compatible with a finite resource supply.

Its warning is conditional: without major changes in resource use and environmental destruction, population and industrial capacity may decline abruptly.

Exponential growth means a repeated proportional increase. The report asks how this can continue with finite resources if resource use and environmental damage do not change enough.

DHSS

Can consumption be maintained despite depletion?

The Dasgupta–Heal–Solow–Stiglitz (DHSS) model responds to the Meadows report. Its objective: maintain consumption per person despite declining resource use.

Substitution

Replace part of the scarce resource with other inputs.

Technical progress

Make each unit of the resource more productive.

Increasing returns

A larger scale of production can generate a more than proportionate increase in output.

1 · Input substitution

Production can replace some of the scarce resource with other inputs. How much it can replace depends on the production technology.

2 · Resource-augmenting technical progress

Technical progress can make each unit of the resource more useful in production. A smaller physical quantity may then provide more productive services.

3 · Increasing returns to scale

Production can rise more than proportionally when its scale increases. This is the third mechanism identified in the course.

Production

Adding land and an exhaustible resource

Nordhaus adds fixed land and an exhaustible resource to the Solow production function:

$$Y=K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}$$

Goal: rewrite the same production function so that products become sums. All inputs are positive where we take logarithms; the exponents are constants.

1 · Take the natural logarithm of both sides
$$\ln Y=\ln\!\left[K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right]$$

$\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm. At this stage, we only apply the same operation to both sides.

2 · Turn the product into a sum

Use $\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v$ on the four multiplied factors:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\ln(K^\alpha)+\ln(T^\beta)\\&+\ln(R^\gamma)+\ln\!\left[(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right].\end{aligned}$$
3 · Bring each power in front of its logarithm

Use $\ln(u^a)=a\ln u$. For example, $\ln(K^\alpha)=\alpha\ln K$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\ln(AL).\end{aligned}$$
4 · Split effective labour into A and L
$$\ln(AL)=\ln A+\ln L$$
$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(\ln A+\ln L).\end{aligned}$$

Every term comes from one factor of the original function. We have rewritten output; we have not differentiated yet.

$Y$, $K$, $L$

Output, capital and labour.

$A$ and $AL$

Labour-augmenting productivity and effective labour.

$T$

Land: its quantity is fixed.

$S$ and $R$

The remaining resource stock and the extraction flow used in production.

The resource stock and the resource flow

$S$ is the stock left. $R$ is the amount used per unit of time. Production uses $R$; extraction reduces $S$.

The production exponents

$\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are the powers on capital, land and resource use. The remaining power, $1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$, applies to effective labour.

$$\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=g_A,\qquad \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}=n,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}=0$$

Productivity

$A$ grows at the given rate $g_A$.

Population

$L$ grows at rate $n$.

Land

$T$ does not grow.

Resource use

A constant proportional extraction rate

$$\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS,\qquad \frac{1}{S}\frac{dS}{dt}=-q$$

Assumption

The extraction rate $q$ is constant: each period uses the same fraction of the remaining stock.

Mechanism

A smaller stock means a smaller extraction flow, even with the same proportional rate.

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Start from the extraction rule $R=qS$, with $q$ constant.

1 · Differentiate the extraction rule
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=q\frac{dS}{dt}=q(-qS)$$
2 · Use R = qS
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=-qR\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Both the stock and the extraction flow decline at the same proportional rate.

Fixing $q$ simplifies the model. Choosing resource use optimally across dates is the problem introduced by Hotelling.

Growth

The economy’s regular growth rate

Each input contributes its growth rate, weighted by its production exponent. Land does not grow; resource use grows at $-q$.

$$g_Y=\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
1 · Why differentiate a logarithm?

Each variable depends on time. The chain rule gives the derivative of $\ln X(t)$:

$$\frac{d\ln X(t)}{dt}=\frac{1}{X(t)}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}\equiv g_X.$$

$dX/dt$ is the change in the level. Dividing by $X$ makes it a proportional growth rate.

2 · Differentiate the logged production function

The exponents are constant, so they remain in front of the derivatives. Apply the same chain rule to every logarithm:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt}={}&\alpha\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}+\beta\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}\\&+\gamma\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\left(\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}+\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}\right).\end{aligned}$$
3 · Replace each proportional derivative by its name
$$g_Y=\alpha g_K+\beta g_T+\gamma g_R+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+g_L)$$

This is why each input’s growth rate is multiplied by its production exponent.

4 · Substitute the model’s assumptions
$$g_T=0,\qquad g_R=-q,\qquad g_L=n$$
$$\begin{aligned}g_Y={}&\alpha g_K+\beta(0)+\gamma(-q)\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n).\end{aligned}$$

Fixed land removes the $\beta g_T$ term. Declining resource use produces the negative term $-\gamma q$.

With a constant saving rate $s$ and capital depreciation $\delta$:

$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
1 · Divide every term of accumulation by K
$$\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=\frac{sY}{K}-\frac{\delta K}{K}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$

The left side is $g_K$. Capital cancels in the depreciation term.

2 · Isolate the output–capital ratio
$$\frac{Y}{K}=\frac{g_K+\delta}{s}$$

On a regular growth path, $g_K$ is constant. With constant $s>0$ and $\delta$, the right side is constant, so $Y/K$ is constant.

3 · Differentiate the logarithm of that constant ratio
$$\ln(Y/K)=\ln Y-\ln K$$
$$0=\frac{d\ln(Y/K)}{dt}=g_Y-g_K$$
$$g_Y=g_K$$

The zero comes from differentiating a constant. Output and capital must therefore grow at the same proportional rate.

1

Regular growth

Capital grows at a constant rate.

2

Constant output–capital ratio

$Y/K$ stays constant.

3

Same growth rate

Output and capital grow together: $g_Y=g_K$.

$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Substitute gK = gY
$$g_Y=\alpha g_Y-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
2 · Subtract αgY from both sides
$$g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$
$$(1-\alpha)g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$

Factor out $g_Y$ on the left: $g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha)g_Y$.

3 · Divide both sides by 1 − α
$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
4 · Read the condition for positive growth

Since $0<\alpha<1$, the denominator is positive. The fraction is positive exactly when its numerator is positive:

$$\begin{aligned}g_Y>0\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q>0\\&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q.\end{aligned}$$
Positive growth

The contribution of effective labour must exceed the loss from resource depletion.

$$(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q$$

If depletion is too fast, the condition fails.

Living standards

Growth of GDP per person

From total GDP to GDP per person

Subtract population growth: $g_Y-n$.

$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · GDP per person is Y divided by L
$$\ln(Y/L)=\ln Y-\ln L$$
$$\frac{d\ln(Y/L)}{dt}=g_Y-g_L=g_Y-n$$

The subtraction comes from the quotient $Y/L$. It is the growth rate of that quotient, not a subtraction from the level of output.

2 · Give both terms the same denominator
$$n=\frac{n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q-n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
3 · Expand and collect only the terms in n
$$\begin{aligned}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)n-n(1-\alpha)\\&\quad=n-\alpha n-\beta n-\gamma n-n+\alpha n\\&\quad=-\beta n-\gamma n=-(\beta+\gamma)n.\end{aligned}$$

The $n$ terms cancel, and so do the $\alpha n$ terms. The terms involving $g_A$ and $q$ are unchanged.

4 · Put the remaining terms together
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$

This is the per-capita growth formula shown above.

Condition to retain

Productivity must grow fast enough to offset population growth and the declining resource flow.

Growth drag

The drag on economic growth

The growth drag is the reduction in per-capita growth caused by the resource constraints.

Comparison path

Land and resource use grow with population.

Constrained path

Land is fixed and resource use declines.

$$\mathrm{drag}=\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Write growth on the comparison path

In this comparison, land and resource use both grow with population: $g_T=g_R=n$. Use the same production function and regular-growth condition $g_K=g_Y$:

$$\begin{aligned}(1-\alpha)g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}={}&\beta n+\gamma n\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A+(1-\alpha)n.\end{aligned}$$
2 · Convert the comparison to per-capita growth
$$g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}$$

Divide the previous line by $1-\alpha$, then subtract $n$. Its population term cancels.

3 · Subtract constrained growth from comparison growth
$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{drag}={}&\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}\\&-\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}\\={}&\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}.\end{aligned}$$

The productivity terms cancel. The minus sign before the second fraction turns its two negative terms into positive terms.

Reading the sign

The drag is a positive gap: it measures how much the constraints reduce growth.

Substitution

Why the elasticity of substitution matters

Elasticity = 1

Cobb–Douglas allows substitution between the resource and other inputs.

Elasticity < 1

Replacing the scarce resource is more difficult.

1

Scarcity rises

The resource flow becomes smaller.

2

The resource claims more income

Its income share tends towards one; the other factor shares become small.

3

The growth drag increases

The drag tends towards $n+q$; GDP per person declines at rate $n+q$.

1 · A resource becomes scarcer

The production process has access to a progressively smaller resource flow.

2 · Replacement is difficult

An elasticity below one limits the ability to compensate for that smaller flow by changing the mix of inputs.

3 · The growth constraint becomes stronger

The resource absorbs a larger income share, and its scarcity imposes an increasing drag on growth. The limiting outcome described is a per-capita growth rate of $-(n+q)$.

Hotelling

Choosing resource use across time

Starting point

The resource’s income share is constant, while the quantity used falls. The resource price rises.

What is missing?

Fixing extraction $q$ and the saving rate does not establish an optimal allocation.

Hotelling’s problem: choose consumption and resource use together, accounting for capital accumulation and depletion.

$$\max\int_0^\infty\frac{C(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=Y-C-\delta K,\qquad \frac{dS}{dt}=-R$$
$$Y=K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}$$
Consumption and discounting

$C$ is consumption. Utility is added across time. The factor $e^{-\rho t}$ discounts future utility. $\rho$ measures impatience; $\varepsilon$ controls the curvature of utility.

Capital accumulation

Output left after consumption and depreciation adds to capital.

Resource depletion

Using $R$ units reduces the remaining resource stock by the same amount. This production function has no separate land input.

Hotelling exercise

Two controls, two states, four conditions

CONTROL VARIABLES · choices

$C$ = consumption.
$R$ = resource use.
Choose their values at each date.

STATE VARIABLES · stocks

$K$ = capital.
$S$ = resource left.
They change through accumulation and extraction.

The current-value Hamiltonian adds current utility and the value of changes in both stocks:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal H={}&\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\\&+\lambda_K\left[K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}-C-\delta K\right]\\&-\lambda_SR.\end{aligned}$$

Capital

$\lambda_K$: the shadow value of one more unit of capital.

Resource

$\lambda_S$: the shadow value of one more unit left in the ground.

A shadow value measures how much an extra unit of a stock is worth in this optimisation problem.

A · FOCs for CONTROL VARIABLES: C and R

For each control, set its partial derivative to zero: $\partial\mathcal H/\partial C=0$ and $\partial\mathcal H/\partial R=0$.

1 · Consumption C
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial C}=C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0$$
Meaning

The gain from consuming one more unit equals the value of keeping that unit as capital: $C^{-\varepsilon}=\lambda_K$.

Differentiate the two terms containing C
$$\frac{d}{dC}\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{\partial(-\lambda_K C)}{\partial C}=-\lambda_K$$
Set the derivative to zero
$$C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_K=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
2 · Resource use R
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S=0$$
Meaning

Using one more unit of the resource creates output worth $\lambda_K\gamma Y/R$, but removes a unit from the stock worth $\lambda_S$. At the optimum, gain = cost.

Differentiate production
$$\frac{\partial Y}{\partial R}=\gamma K^\alpha R^{\gamma-1}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}=\gamma\frac{Y}{R}$$
Include the loss from extraction
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S$$
$$\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}=\lambda_S$$

B · Conditions for STATE VARIABLES: K and S

Use the costate equations for their shadow values. Do not set the stock derivatives of $\mathcal H$ to zero.

3 · Capital stock K
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K$$
Meaning

The value of capital changes with impatience $\rho$, depreciation $\delta$ and its marginal product $\alpha Y/K$.

Differentiate the Hamiltonian with respect to K
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}=\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)$$
Use the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\rho\lambda_K-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}&=\rho\lambda_K-\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)\\&=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K.\end{aligned}$$
4 · Resource stock S
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Meaning

The Hamiltonian contains the extraction flow $R$, but no direct term in the remaining stock $S$. Therefore $\partial\mathcal H/\partial S=0$.

Apply the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}$$
Use the absence of a direct S term
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Divide by the shadow value to obtain its growth rate
$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$
Result to remember

The resource’s current-value shadow price grows at the rate of time preference $\rho$.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$

Economic meaning

Keeping a unit in the ground allows it to be used later. Its future shadow value must compensate for the delay in utility.

Why the stock still matters

Extraction removes future resources. This cost is recorded by $-\lambda_SR$, even though $S$ does not appear directly in the Hamiltonian.

Which price?

This result concerns $\lambda_S$, the shadow value in this exercise.

Historical discussion

Wood, coal and the timing of resource use

The shift from wood to coal is presented alongside deforestation. Two interpretations explain the connection:

First interpretation: scarcity prompts innovation

Wood is exhausted through deforestation, creating the need to find another energy source. Coal combustion then provides that alternative.

Second interpretation: the alternative changes scarcity

Coal use is already known but initially costs more than wood. Once coal becomes an available alternative, energy supply depends less on scarce wood.

The incentive to preserve forests weakens, so deforestation can occur. This is the interpretation of Dasgupta and Stiglitz.

Story 1: wood depletion leads to the search for coal. Story 2: access to coal reduces the incentive to preserve wood. The direction of the explanation is different.

Discussion: electricity storage and oil

If the second story applies to electricity storage and oil, what could happen? Is this likely? What could prevent the problem? The support leaves these questions open.

Natural resources in endogenous growth models

AK model

Why the resource constraint ends permanent AK growth

Add the resource flow $R$ to AK production, with $0<\gamma<1$ and a constant saving rate $s$:

$$Y=AKR^\gamma$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS$$
$$g_K=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
1 · Divide accumulation by capital
$$g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
2 · Insert resource-dependent production
$$g_K=s\frac{AKR^\gamma}{K}-\delta=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
3 · Read the effect of declining resource use

As $R$ falls, $R^\gamma$ falls too. The positive term gets smaller, while depreciation remains. Eventually $g_K$ is negative and approaches $-\delta$.

Result

The declining resource term eventually outweighs accumulation. Permanent AK growth disappears; capital and production contract.

One result to retain

Innovation can offset resource depletion

Positive growth is feasible when one or more of these conditions hold:

More efficient research

Research produces innovations more effectively.

Larger innovations

Each success creates a larger technological improvement.

Slower depletion

A smaller $q$ means the resource is used up more slowly.

Remember

Better research, bigger innovations and/or slower depletion.

The DICE model and climate policy

DICE

Linking the world economy and the climate

DICE — the Dynamic Integrated Climate–Economy model — extends Ramsey by linking the world economy and the climate.

1

Production

Economic activity generates greenhouse-gas emissions.

2

Climate change

Emissions affect the climate.

3

Economic damage

Climate change harms economic agents. This is a pollution externality.

Private production decisions

Private producers do not fully include the climate damage imposed on others.

The social planner’s perspective

The planner includes damage to everyone. In the comparison presented, the planner chooses less production and fewer emissions than firms left to themselves.

The policy implication

A positive carbon tax makes firms take the social cost of emissions into account.

The policy problem

Choose emissions control to maximise welfare over time, accounting for climate damage.

Objective

Discounted economic welfare

Policies are chosen to maximise economic welfare over time:

$$W=\int_{2020}\frac{c(t)^{1-\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}L(t)e^{-(\rho+pr+ad)}$$
1

Utility from consumption

$c(t)^{1-\epsilon}/(1-\epsilon)$ measures utility per person.

2

Multiply by population

$L(t)$ turns utility per person into welfare for the population.

3

Apply discounting and add across time

The exponential represents discounting. The integral combines welfare across dates, starting in 2020.

Consumption

$c(t)$ is consumption per person.

Population

$L(t)$ is the number of people and the labour force.

Substitution over time

$1/\epsilon$ is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution: willingness to shift consumption between dates.

The three discounting terms

Time preference · ρ

$\rho$ measures the preference for utility sooner rather than later.

Precaution · pr

Reflects uncertainty about consumption growth. In the course, this adjustment reduces the discount rate.

Climate risk · ad

Adjusts for climate-investment risk that diversification cannot remove. It increases the discount rate.

Discounting sets the weight of future welfare compared with current welfare. The course combines pure time preference with the precautionary and climate-risk adjustments.

The combined discount rate changes over time. The precautionary term makes it decline. Some scenarios also impose extra constraints.

Calibration stated in the slide

The near-term discount rate is about 4.5% per year. It falls over time because of the precautionary term.

About the slide’s notation

The full expression above follows the supplied slide. Its upper integration limit, integration differential and time dependence inside the discount factor are not written. The text says the discount rate varies over time; the missing notation is not reconstructed here.

Production

Output after climate damage and abatement costs

$$Y_t=(1-\Lambda(t))(1-\Omega(t))A(t)K(t)^\alpha L(t)^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1$$

$\Omega(t)$ · Damage

The output loss caused by climate change.

$\Lambda(t)$ · Abatement

The resources used to reduce emissions.

Production before the two deductions

$A(t)K(t)^\alpha L(t)^{1-\alpha}$ combines productivity, capital and labour.

Climate damage

Multiplying by $1-\Omega(t)$ keeps the share of output left after climate damage.

Abatement costs

The factor $1-\Lambda(t)$ keeps the share left after paying for emissions control. Reducing emissions uses resources.

Damage function

Temperature change and output losses

$$\Omega(t)=\Phi_1T_{AT}(t)+\Phi_2T_{AT}(t)^2$$

$T_{AT}(t)$

Temperature change relative to the preindustrial level.

$\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$

Coefficients that translate warming into an output loss.

Reading the function

A temperature term and a squared-temperature term describe how damage responds to warming.

Abatement

The cost of controlling emissions

$$\Lambda(t)=\theta_1(t)\mu(t)^{\theta_2}$$

$\mu(t)$

The emissions-control rate: the degree of emissions reduction.

$\theta_1(t)$

The fraction of output needed to reduce emissions to zero.

$\theta_2$

The shape of the relationship between emissions control and its cost.

A backstop technology would allow emissions to be reduced to zero. Its price determines the cost of a zero-emissions policy.

Technological improvement

The model assumes that technologies for zero emissions improve over time, so the backstop cost falls.

Emissions

From production to greenhouse-gas emissions

Add production-related and land-related CO₂ emissions, then apply the emissions-control rate:

$$ECO_2(t)=\bigl(\sigma(t)Y(t)+ECO_{2,\mathrm{land}}(t)\bigr)(1-\mu(t))$$
Production-related emissions

$\sigma(t)$ is the emissions intensity multiplying output $Y(t)$. The product $\sigma(t)Y(t)$ is the production-related component.

Land-related emissions

$ECO_{2,\mathrm{land}}(t)$ adds emissions associated with land.

Emissions after control

The factor $1-\mu(t)$ is the fraction remaining after applying emissions control to the quantity in parentheses.

Other greenhouse gases use a baseline emissions term and the same control factor:

$$ECO_{2,\mathrm{nonCO_2}}(t)=ECO_{2,\mathrm{nonCO_2base}}(1-\mu(t))$$
Scenarios

Different policy questions, different simulations

The numerical simulations change the policy choice, a constraint or a model assumption.

Keep existing climate policies and the assumed trend in the carbon price.

Choose the emissions-control path $\mu(t)$ to maximise welfare, weighing abatement costs against avoided damage.

Maximise welfare subject to a temperature ceiling. The ceiling restricts the admissible policy paths.

Use alternative constant discount rates to change the weight assigned to future welfare.

Use a damage function with larger output losses for a given warming. The Howard–Sterner specification is the alternative considered.

Extend Paris Accord commitments over a longer horizon.

Implementation constraint

The emissions-control rate can only increase at a limited speed.

SCC

The social cost of carbon

Definition

The social cost of carbon (SCC) is the monetary value of the damage caused by one additional tonne of CO₂.

It is used in benefit–cost analysis. Estimating it combines socioeconomic projections, climate models, damage functions and discounting.

Socioeconomic projections

Probabilistic projections describe possible future economic and demographic conditions.

Climate models

Climate models relate emissions to changes in the climate.

Damage functions

Damage functions translate climate changes into consequences for society and the economy.

Discounting methods

Discounting determines the weight given to damage occurring at different future dates.

Model differences

Why do damage estimates differ so much?

1 · Which impacts are counted?

Temperature only, or also precipitation changes and more intense extreme weather? Coverage changes the estimated loss.

2 · How rapidly do losses increase?

Estimates differ on how damage intensifies with warming. Future adaptation, or its absence, remains a source of uncertainty.

Coverage: which climate effects are counted. Intensity: how much the losses rise as warming increases.

3 · Does the shock change output’s level or its growth rate?

Level effects

Climate change reduces the level of output. Once the climate stabilises, growth returns to its old rate, but the output loss remains.

Growth effects

The climate shock changes the growth rate itself. That rate stays different, usually lower, unless the climate returns to its earlier state.

Level effect: lower output, then the old growth rate. Growth effect: a lasting change in the growth rate. Long-run losses therefore differ greatly.

Climate policy with directed technical change

Directed innovation

Innovation responds to economic incentives

Exogenous technology

DICE takes technological change as given. The policy response described is a Pigovian emissions tax that increases over time.

Endogenous technology

With directed technical change (DTC), incentives determine which technologies improve. Research responds to profitability.

Research can improve different technologies: save energy or replace a polluting input with a cleaner one. When firms choose the direction of research, the policy answer can change.

Complementary inputs

Energy or fossil-fuel resources are difficult to replace with capital or labour. Innovation is studied as a way of saving the energy or resource needed for production.

Substitutable inputs

Clean and dirty inputs can replace each other, as with renewable and fossil-fuel sources of electricity. The AABH model studies innovation towards the clean alternative.

The substitutable-input case produces the sharper change in policy recommendations.

AABH

Four policy messages to be explained

AABH: Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn and Hémous.

Act early

Intervention may need to be immediate and decisive.

Act temporarily

Support may only be needed for a limited period.

Limit growth costs

The long-run growth cost may be limited.

Combine instruments

Optimal regulation needs a tax and research subsidies.

The four messages address timing, duration, growth costs and policy instruments. Their mechanisms are developed in the model below.

These conclusions depend on the model’s conditions.

Production

Clean and dirty inputs

An infinite horizon in discrete time. Competitive final-good producers combine clean input $Y_c$ and dirty input $Y_d$:

$$Y=\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$
Key assumption

$\varepsilon>1$: clean and dirty inputs can substitute for each other. The elasticity measures how easily they do so.

Each sector $j=c,d$ combines labour $L_j$ with machines $x_{ji}$, indexed by $i$ from zero to one:

$$Y_j=L_j^\beta\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
The sector index j

$j=c$ means the clean sector; $j=d$ means the dirty sector. Both use the same type of production function.

The machine index i

$i$ identifies a machine. $x_{ji}$ is its quantity and $A_{ji}$ its quality. The integral adds the machines’ contributions.

Market structure

The final good and both inputs are produced competitively. Each machine is supplied by a monopolist and made using the final good.

Innovation

Scientists improve machine quality

1

Choose a sector

Scientists choose clean or dirty research. Their total mass is one.

2

Receive an assignment

Each scientist is randomly assigned to a machine in that sector; $s_{jt}$ is the sector’s scientist share.

Success probability

$h_j$.

Quality improvement

A success multiplies quality by $1+\gamma$, with $\gamma>0$.

Private reward

Monopoly rights last one period.

$$A_{jit}=\begin{cases}(1+\gamma)A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if successful},\\A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if unsuccessful}.\end{cases}$$

Average sector quality then evolves as follows:

$$A_{jt}=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Identify the fraction of machines improved

Research effort is $s_{jt}$ and its success probability is $h_j$. With random assignment, the fraction improved is $h_js_{jt}$; the fraction unchanged is $1-h_js_{jt}$.

2 · Add unchanged and improved quality
$$\begin{aligned}A_{jt}={}&(1-h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}\\&+h_js_{jt}(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

Random assignment lets both groups start from the same inherited average quality. Successful research multiplies that quality by $1+\gamma$.

3 · Factor out inherited quality and simplify
$$\begin{aligned}A_{jt}&=\left[1-h_js_{jt}+h_js_{jt}+\gamma h_js_{jt}\right]A_{j,t-1}\\&=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The terms $-h_js_{jt}$ and $+h_js_{jt}$ cancel. The remaining addition is the quality gain.

Machines

Machine demand, monopoly quantity and profit

Machine demand equals the machine’s marginal productivity, valued at the sector price $p_j$:

$$p_{ji}=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$
1 · Differentiate the contribution of machine i

Hold labour, technology and the other machine quantities fixed. In the production integral, the quantity of this machine appears as $x_{ji}^{1-\beta}$:

$$\frac{d(x_{ji}^{1-\beta})}{dx_{ji}}=(1-\beta)x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$
2 · Multiply by the fixed factors and sector price
$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=(1-\beta)L_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$
$$p_{ji}=p_j\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$

$p_j$ converts the extra sector output into revenue. The factor $1-\beta$ comes directly from the power rule.

Choice

The monopolist chooses the machine quantity to maximise profit.

Cost

Each machine unit costs $1-\beta$ units of final good. The final-good price is one.

The resulting quantity and profit are:

$$x_{ji}=\bigl[(1-\beta)p_j\bigr]^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta\bigl[(1-\beta)p_j\bigr]^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$
1 · Write profit as a function of machine quantity
$$\begin{aligned}\pi_{ji}(x_{ji})&=p_{ji}(x_{ji})x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}\\&=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}.\end{aligned}$$

Revenue is price times quantity. Cost is the unit cost $1-\beta$ times quantity.

2 · Differentiate and set the derivative to zero
$$\frac{d\pi_{ji}}{dx_{ji}}=(1-\beta)^2p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}-(1-\beta)=0$$

Differentiating $x_{ji}^{1-\beta}$ adds a second factor $1-\beta$. Divide the condition by $1-\beta>0$:

$$(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}=1$$
3 · Isolate the machine quantity
$$x_{ji}^\beta=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta$$
$$x_{ji}=\bigl[(1-\beta)p_j\bigr]^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$

Multiply by $x_{ji}^\beta$, then raise both sides to $1/\beta$.

4 · Insert the optimal quantity into profit

The left side of the simplified first-order condition is exactly $p_{ji}$. At the chosen quantity, $p_{ji}=1$, so:

$$\pi_{ji}=x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}=\beta x_{ji}$$
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta\bigl[(1-\beta)p_j\bigr]^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$

These coefficients use the production function and unit cost retained in this course.

The relevant output price

$p_j$ is the sector-output price. $p_{ji}$ is the price of one machine type.

The scale of machine demand

Machine use depends on the sector price, labour $L_j$ and machine quality $A_{ji}$.

Monopoly profit

$\pi_{ji}$ is the machine producer’s profit. Under the stated unit cost, it equals $\beta$ times the chosen machine quantity.

Sector output

Input production at the machine quantities chosen

Insert the chosen machine quantities into the sector production function:

$$Y_j=\bigl[(1-\beta)p_j\bigr]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j$$
1 · Start from the sector production function
$$Y_j=L_j^\beta\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}\,di$$

Insert $x_{ji}=[(1-\beta)p_j]^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$.

2 · Apply the power 1 − β to every factor
$$x_{ji}^{1-\beta}=[(1-\beta)p_j]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j^{1-\beta}A_{ji}^{1-\beta}$$
3 · Collect equal bases and take common factors outside
$$L_j^\beta L_j^{1-\beta}=L_j,\qquad A_{ji}^\beta A_{ji}^{1-\beta}=A_{ji}$$
$$Y_j=[(1-\beta)p_j]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di$$

The price and labour input do not depend on the machine index $i$, so they come outside the integral.

4 · Use average machine quality
$$A_j\equiv\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di$$

The machine interval has total length one, so this integral is average quality. Therefore:

$$Y_j=[(1-\beta)p_j]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j$$
From machines to the sector

$A_j$ is average machine quality. Sector output depends on its price, labour input and technology.

The integral combines all machines, so index $i$ disappears. Index $j$ remains because the formula applies separately to the clean and dirty sectors.

Research reward

Expected profit from choosing a research sector

If research succeeds

Expected quality is $(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}$.

Expected reward

Weight the monopoly profit from the improved technology by the probability of success.

$$\Pi_{jt}=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta\bigl[(1-\beta)p_{jt}\bigr]^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Start from the machine producer’s profit
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{ji}$$
2 · Use quality after a successful innovation

Random assignment gives inherited average quality $A_{j,t-1}$. Conditional on success, multiply it by $1+\gamma$:

$$\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}=\beta[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{1/\beta}L_{jt}(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}$$
3 · Weight this reward by success probability
$$\begin{aligned}\Pi_{jt}&=h_j\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}\\&=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The expected reward includes the monopoly right obtained if research succeeds.

Research success

$h_j$ weights the reward by the probability of obtaining an innovation.

Quality after success

$(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}$ is the improved technology associated with successful research.

The market for that technology

The sector’s price $p_{jt}$ and labour input $L_{jt}$ enter the monopoly reward. The scientist compares this expected reward across the two possible sectors.

Equivalently, using the value of sector output:

$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta(1-\beta)p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}$$
1 · Express inherited quality in terms of current quality
$$A_{j,t-1}=\frac{A_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}$$
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}$$
2 · Match the power appearing in sector output
$$\frac{1}{\beta}=1+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}$$
$$[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{1/\beta}=(1-\beta)p_{jt}[(1-\beta)p_{jt}]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}$$

The last factor, multiplied by $L_{jt}A_{jt}$, is exactly $Y_{jt}$.

3 · Substitute Yjt
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta(1-\beta)p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}$$

The two profit expressions are the same quantity written with different variables.

Research choice

Where will scientists choose to innovate?

Scientists choose the sector with higher expected profit. Compare clean and dirty research:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
1 · Write the numerator and denominator
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c(1+\gamma)\beta[(1-\beta)p_{ct}]^{1/\beta}L_{ct}A_{c,t-1}}{h_d(1+\gamma)\beta[(1-\beta)p_{dt}]^{1/\beta}L_{dt}A_{d,t-1}}$$
2 · Cancel the factors common to both sectors

$1+\gamma$ and $\beta$ cancel. The common factor $1-\beta$ also cancels inside the price ratio:

$$\frac{[(1-\beta)p_{ct}]^{1/\beta}}{[(1-\beta)p_{dt}]^{1/\beta}}=\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}$$
3 · Keep the four sector-specific ratios
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

The terms remaining are success probability, price, labour and inherited quality.

Ratio > 1

Research goes to the clean sector.

Ratio < 1

Research goes to the dirty sector.

Ratio = 1

Scientists may work in both sectors.

Research success probabilities

$h_c/h_d$ compares the probability of a successful clean innovation with that of a successful dirty innovation.

The price effect
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}$$

Relative sector prices enter the reward from innovation. Their direction is determined using the equilibrium price relationship below.

The market-size effect
$$\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}$$

The allocation of labour measures the relative scale of the two input sectors and the corresponding market for machines.

The direct productivity effect
$$\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

An innovation builds on inherited quality. This factor favours the sector that already has the higher technological level.

Equilibrium

Price and market size pull in different directions

Labour allocation is endogenous. Equalise the value of its marginal product across sectors:

$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}$$
1 · Differentiate sector production with respect to labour

Use the original production function, holding the machine quantities fixed in this partial derivative:

$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial L_j}=\beta L_j^{\beta-1}\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}\,di=\beta\frac{Y_j}{L_j}$$

The value of this marginal product is the wage $w_j$:

$$w_j=\beta p_j\frac{Y_j}{L_j}$$
2 · Insert equilibrium sector output
$$\begin{aligned}w_j&=\beta p_j[(1-\beta)p_j]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}A_j\\&=\beta(1-\beta)^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}p_j^{1/\beta}A_j.\end{aligned}$$

The price powers add up: $1+(1-\beta)/\beta=1/\beta$.

3 · Set clean and dirty wages equal

Labour can move across sectors. Equating the two marginal revenues and cancelling their common coefficient gives:

$$p_c^{1/\beta}A_c=p_d^{1/\beta}A_d$$
$$\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{1/\beta}=\frac{A_d}{A_c}$$
4 · Raise both sides to β
$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_d}{A_c}\right)^\beta=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}$$

The negative exponent appears because the technology ratio is inverted.

Price effect → less advanced sector

The more advanced sector has the lower price, which works against its innovation advantage.

The power $-\beta$ is negative: a sector with better technology has a lower price. This price effect reduces its advantage in research profits.

Final-good demand gives the relative input quantities:

$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$
1 · Differentiate with respect to each input

Competitive final-good producers equate input prices with marginal products, taking the final-good price as one. For the clean input:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Y}{\partial Y_c}={}&\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon-1}}\\&\times\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}$$

The two fractions cancel. The bracket to power $1/(\varepsilon-1)$ equals $Y^{1/\varepsilon}$, so:

$$p_c=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon},\qquad p_d=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}.$$
2 · Divide the two marginal products
$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\frac{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}}{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}}=\left(\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}\right)^{-1/\varepsilon}$$

The common factor involving total output cancels.

3 · Isolate the input-quantity ratio

Raise both sides to $-\varepsilon$. The exponents multiply to one: $(-1/\varepsilon)(-\varepsilon)=1$.

$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$

Combine this demand with sector output to obtain labour allocation:

$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1},\qquad\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)>1$$
1 · Divide the sector-output expressions
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}\frac{L_c}{L_d}\frac{A_c}{A_d}$$

The common coefficient involving $1-\beta$ cancels.

2 · Substitute the relative price and multiply exponents
$$\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)}$$
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)+1}\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}$$

The extra $+1$ comes from the technology factor already present in the output ratio.

3 · Express final-good demand using the same technology ratio
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{-\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}$$
4 · Equate the two expressions and isolate labour
$$\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon-\beta}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta(\varepsilon-1)}$$

Dividing powers with the same base subtracts their exponents. Since $\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)$, the exponent is $\sigma-1$.

Market-size effect → more advanced sector

Because $\sigma-1>0$, the more advanced sector attracts more labour.

Laissez-faire

Path dependence in the direction of innovation

Combine the profit ratio with prices, labour allocation and technology accumulation:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
1 · Insert price and labour ratios into research profits
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1}$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-1}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

The two current-technology exponents add: $-1+(\sigma-1)=\sigma-2$. Keep the inherited ratio separate.

2 · Replace current quality using the accumulation equations
$$\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}=\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left[\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right]^{\sigma-2}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
3 · Apply the power to both factors inside the brackets
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^1$$
4 · Add the inherited-technology exponents
$$(\sigma-2)+1=\sigma-1$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$

The exponent on inherited technology is $\sigma-1>0$ in the substitutable-input case.

Which effects dominate?

The direct productivity and market-size effects dominate the price effect. Innovation tends towards the sector already ahead.

Path dependence

When dirty technology starts far enough ahead, research tends to keep improving it. The initial lead shapes future innovation: this is path dependence.

With an initial dirty lead, private research incentives do not automatically redirect innovation towards clean production.

Energy-saving innovation with complementary inputs

Energy-saving DTC

Improving energy services when substitution is difficult

A different route

Improve technologies that save energy or resources. Final output combines a production input $Y_P$ and energy services $Y_E$.

$$Y_t=\left(Y_{Pt}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_{Et}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$
Key assumption

$0<\varepsilon<1$: energy services are difficult to replace with the other input. The inputs are complementary.

$$Y_P=L^\beta\int_0^1 A_{Pi}^{\beta}x_{Pi}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
$$Y_E=E^\beta\int_0^1 A_{Ei}^{\beta}x_{Ei}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
The production input

$Y_P$ uses labour $L$ and its sector’s machines. Their quality is denoted by $A_{Pi}$.

Energy services

$Y_E$ uses the physical resource or energy flow $E$ and energy-sector machines. Their quality $A_{Ei}$ determines the technology with which that physical flow produces energy services.

The innovation question

Innovation can improve either input’s technology. The rest of the framework follows the research-incentive approach used in AABH.

Research incentives

Innovation favours the relatively lagging input

Compare expected profits from production-sector research and energy-sector research, using current technology levels:

$$\frac{\Pi_{Pt}}{\Pi_{Et}}=\frac{h_P}{h_E}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_Es_{Et}}{1+\gamma h_Ps_{Pt}}\right)\left(\frac{LA_{Pt}}{EA_{Et}}\right)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}$$
$$\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1),\qquad 0<\sigma<1$$
1 · Apply the same definition of σ to this case
$$\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)=1-\beta(1-\varepsilon)$$

Here $0<\varepsilon<1$ and $0<\beta<1$. Therefore $0<\beta(1-\varepsilon)<1$, which gives $0<\sigma<1$.

2 · Check the numerator and denominator separately
$$\sigma-1<0,\qquad\sigma>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}<0$$

The numerator is negative and the denominator positive. This explains the sign used when reading the research-profit ratio.

The two research opportunities

$h_P/h_E$ compares research success probabilities. $s_{Pt}$ and $s_{Et}$ are the fractions of scientists working in the two sectors.

Effective input quantities

$LA_{Pt}$ is labour multiplied by production technology, while $EA_{Et}$ is the physical energy flow multiplied by energy-service technology. The final ratio compares these effective quantities.

The role of complementarity

With $0<\sigma<1$, the exponent $(\sigma-1)/\sigma$ is negative. This is the part of the expression associated with innovation towards the relatively lagging input, rather than reinforcement of a technological lead.

Divide numerator and denominator by energy income, then multiply both by $h_P$. This is why $h_P$ appears in the numerator under the notation retained here.

The shale-gas revolution and the energy transition

Shale gas

Coal, gas and clean energy in one model

Acemoglu, Aghion, Barrage and Hémous study a gas boom in a model with coal, gas and clean energy. Innovation responds to profitability.

Final production combines the general input $Y_{Pt}$ and effective electricity $\widetilde A_tE_t$. Climate damage reduces output through $1-D(S_t)$:

$$Y_t=(1-D(S_t))\left((1-\nu)Y_{Pt}^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}+\nu(\widetilde A_tE_t)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}}$$
The general input and electricity

$Y_{Pt}$ is the general production input. $E_t$ is electricity, and $\widetilde A_t$ scales it into effective energy services. The weights $1-\nu$ and $\nu$ determine how the two terms enter the production function.

Limited substitution

$\lambda<1$ is the elasticity of substitution between the general input and electricity. These inputs are difficult to substitute for each other.

Climate damage

The factor $1-D(S_t)$ reduces output according to the damage function evaluated at the environmental state $S_t$.

Electricity can come from three substitutable sources:

$$E_t=\left(\kappa_cE_{ct}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+\kappa_gE_{gt}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+\kappa_sE_{st}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$

$E_{ct}$, $E_{gt}$ and $E_{st}$ are coal, gas and clean electricity. The $\kappa$ terms are their weights. With $\varepsilon>1$, these sources can replace each other more easily than electricity can replace the general input.

Results

Short-run emissions and the long-run transition

Short run: substitution away from coal

A natural-gas boom reduces carbon emissions in the short run by encouraging substitution from coal to gas. This result uses the lower CO₂ emissions of gas per unit of electricity.

Long run: weaker clean innovation

The gas boom discourages clean-energy innovation. It delays, and may even prevent, the transition to zero carbon.

Short run: gas replaces coal. Long run: weaker clean research delays zero-carbon technology. A cleaner mix today can therefore slow the later transition.

Laissez-faire

The gas boom reduces social welfare in the model.
