Chapter 5 — Economic Growth

Growth, Natural Resources and Climate Change

Land and natural resources in a Solow model

Introduction

Growth with finite resources and climate change

Two constraints enter the growth models:

Exhaustible resources

Production uses a resource whose remaining stock falls with extraction.

Climate change

Production affects the climate; climate damage then affects the economy.

Central question

How do resource depletion and climate change alter economic growth?

Starting point

The Limits to Growth: the Meadows report

The problem

The Meadows report asks whether continuing economic and population growth is compatible with a finite resource supply.

Its warning is conditional: without major changes in resource use and environmental destruction, population and industrial capacity may decline abruptly.

Exponential growth means that the economy and population keep increasing proportionally over time. The report places that continuing expansion alongside a finite resource supply. Its central concern is the compatibility of these two features when resource use and environmental damage do not change sufficiently.

DHSS

Can consumption be maintained despite depletion?

The Dasgupta–Heal–Solow–Stiglitz (DHSS) model responds to the Meadows report. Its objective: maintain consumption per person despite declining resource use.

Substitution

Replace part of the scarce resource with other inputs.

Technical progress

Make each unit of the resource more productive.

Increasing returns

A larger scale of production can generate a more than proportionate increase in output.

1 · Input substitution

Production can adjust its combination of inputs, replacing some of the scarce resource with other production factors. The possibility of this adjustment depends on the production technology.

2 · Resource-augmenting technical progress

Technical progress can make the resource used in production more effective. A smaller physical resource input may then provide more productive services than before.

3 · Increasing returns to scale

An increase in the scale of production can generate a more than proportionate increase in output. This is the third technological mechanism identified as a possible counterweight to declining resource use.

Production

Adding land and an exhaustible resource

Nordhaus adds fixed land and an exhaustible resource to the Solow production function:

$$Y=K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}$$

$Y$, $K$, $L$

Output, capital and labour.

$A$ and $AL$

Labour-augmenting productivity and effective labour.

$T$

Land: its quantity is fixed.

$S$ and $R$

The remaining resource stock and the extraction flow used in production.

The resource stock and the resource flow

$S$ is what remains available. $R$ is what is used per unit of time. Production depends on the flow $R$, while extraction changes the stock $S$. Keeping these two quantities distinct is essential for reading the model.

The production exponents

The exponents $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ determine how capital, land and resource use enter the Cobb–Douglas production function. The remaining exponent, $1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$, applies to effective labour.

$$\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=g_A,\qquad \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}=n,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}=0$$

Productivity

$A$ grows at the given rate $g_A$.

Population

$L$ grows at rate $n$.

Land

$T$ does not grow.

Resource use

A constant proportional extraction rate

$$\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS,\qquad \frac{1}{S}\frac{dS}{dt}=-q$$

Assumption

The extraction rate $q$ is constant: each period uses the same fraction of the remaining stock.

Mechanism

A smaller stock means a smaller extraction flow, even with the same proportional rate.

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Start from the extraction rule $R=qS$, with $q$ constant.

1 · Differentiate the extraction rule
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=q\frac{dS}{dt}=q(-qS)$$
2 · Use R = qS
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=-qR\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Both the stock and the extraction flow decline at the same proportional rate.

Fixing $q$ simplifies the model. Choosing resource use optimally across dates is the problem introduced by Hotelling.

Growth

The economy’s regular growth rate

Each input contributes its growth rate, weighted by its production exponent. Land does not grow; resource use grows at $-q$.

$$g_Y=\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
1 · Weight each input’s growth rate by its exponent
$$g_Y=\alpha g_K+\beta g_T+\gamma g_R+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+g_L)$$
2 · Insert the assumptions
$$g_T=0,\qquad g_R=-q,\qquad g_L=n$$
$$g_Y=\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$

The negative term $-\gamma q$ is the contribution of declining resource use.

With a constant saving rate $s$ and capital depreciation $\delta$:

$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
1

Regular growth

Capital grows at a constant rate.

2

Constant output–capital ratio

$Y/K$ stays constant.

3

Same growth rate

Output and capital grow together: $g_Y=g_K$.

$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Replace capital growth by output growth
$$g_Y=\alpha g_Y-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
2 · Collect the terms involving gY
$$(1-\alpha)g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$
3 · Divide by 1 − α
$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
Positive growth

The contribution of effective labour must exceed the loss from resource depletion.

$$(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q$$

If depletion is too fast, the condition fails.

Living standards

Growth of GDP per person

From total GDP to GDP per person

Subtract population growth: $g_Y-n$.

$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Put both terms over the same denominator
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q-n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
2 · Collect the terms in n
$$(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)n-(1-\alpha)n=-(\beta+\gamma)n$$
3 · Read the resulting numerator

Productivity growth contributes positively. Population growth and resource depletion enter with negative signs in this per-capita expression.

Condition to retain

Productivity must grow fast enough to offset population growth and the declining resource flow.

Growth drag

The drag on economic growth

The growth drag is the reduction in per-capita growth caused by the resource constraints.

Comparison path

Land and resource use grow with population.

Constrained path

Land is fixed and resource use declines.

$$\mathrm{drag}=\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
Reading the sign

The drag is a positive gap: it measures how much the constraints reduce growth.

Substitution

Why the elasticity of substitution matters

Elasticity = 1

Cobb–Douglas allows substitution between the resource and other inputs.

Elasticity < 1

Replacing the scarce resource is more difficult.

1

Scarcity rises

The resource flow becomes smaller.

2

The resource claims more income

Its income share tends towards one; the other factor shares become small.

3

The growth drag increases

The drag tends towards $n+q$; GDP per person declines at rate $n+q$.

1 · A resource becomes scarcer

The production process has access to a progressively smaller resource flow.

2 · Replacement is difficult

An elasticity below one limits the ability to compensate for that smaller flow by changing the mix of inputs.

3 · The growth constraint becomes stronger

The resource absorbs a larger income share, and its scarcity imposes an increasing drag on growth. The limiting outcome described is a per-capita growth rate of $-(n+q)$.

Hotelling

Choosing resource use across time

Starting point

The resource’s income share is constant, while the quantity used falls. The resource price rises.

What is missing?

Fixing extraction $q$ and the saving rate does not establish an optimal allocation.

Hotelling’s problem: choose consumption and resource use together, accounting for capital accumulation and depletion.

$$\max\int_0^\infty\frac{C(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=Y-C-\delta K,\qquad \frac{dS}{dt}=-R$$
$$Y=K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}$$
Consumption and discounting

$C$ is consumption. Its utility is added across dates, with $e^{-\rho t}$ giving less weight to later utility. The parameter $\rho$ is the rate of time preference; $\varepsilon$ determines the curvature of consumption utility.

Capital accumulation

Output left after consumption and depreciation adds to capital. The first constraint records that allocation.

Resource depletion

Using $R$ units of the resource reduces the remaining stock by the same amount. The production function in this problem includes the resource flow and no separate land term.

Current utility

The first term is the utility from current consumption.

The capital constraint

The bracket multiplied by $\lambda_K$ is the capital-accumulation equation. The multiplier represents the implicit value attached to capital in this optimisation problem.

Historical discussion

Wood, coal and the timing of resource use

The shift from wood to coal is presented alongside deforestation. Two interpretations explain the connection:

First interpretation: scarcity prompts innovation

Wood is exhausted through deforestation, creating the need to find another energy source. Coal combustion then provides that alternative.

Second interpretation: the alternative changes scarcity

Coal use is already known but initially costs more than wood. Once coal becomes an available alternative, energy supply depends less on scarce wood.

The incentive to preserve forests weakens, so deforestation can occur. This is the interpretation of Dasgupta and Stiglitz.

The first story puts depletion before the search for an alternative. The second puts knowledge of an alternative before depletion, and asks how that alternative changes the incentive to conserve the original resource. The same historical association is therefore given two different explanations.

Discussion: electricity storage and oil

If the second interpretation applies, what might happen once electricity storage becomes available? Is that outcome likely? What could prevent the tragedy? These are open discussion questions in the supplied material; no proposed answer is added.

Natural resources in endogenous growth models

AK model

Why the resource constraint ends permanent AK growth

Add the resource flow $R$ to AK production, with $0<\gamma<1$ and a constant saving rate $s$:

$$Y=AKR^\gamma$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS$$
$$g_K=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
1 · Divide accumulation by capital
$$g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
2 · Insert resource-dependent production
$$g_K=s\frac{AKR^\gamma}{K}-\delta=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
3 · Read the effect of declining resource use

As $R$ falls, $R^\gamma$ becomes smaller. The positive accumulation term shrinks, while depreciation remains. Eventually $g_K$ becomes negative and tends towards $-\delta$.

Result

The declining resource term eventually outweighs accumulation. Permanent AK growth disappears; capital and production contract.

Schumpeterian growth

Production, research and resource depletion

Add an exhaustible resource to the creation–destruction model:

$$Y=(AL)^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha R^\beta$$
$$\frac{dS}{dt}=-R,\qquad L+L_R=1$$

Production labour

$L$ produces goods.

Research labour

$L_R$ improves technology. Total labour is normalised to one, so $L_R<1$.

Resource flow

$R$ is the natural resource used in production.

$$R=qS\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{dR}{dt}=-qR$$

The labour constraint divides a fixed workforce between producing goods and improving technology. The resource equation specifies the decline in the physical input available for production. Innovation changes $A$; extraction changes $R$. The model examines their combined effect on output.

Market equilibrium

Intermediate production and final output

Production cost

One unit of intermediate input requires one unit of final good.

Numeraire

The final-good price equals one.

Input demand

Competitive final-good producers pay the intermediate input its marginal productivity.

$$p(x)=\alpha(AL)^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}R^\beta$$
The producer’s choice

Maximise $\pi(x)=p(x)x-x$: revenue minus production cost. The resulting quantity and output are:

$$x=\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}ALR^{\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$Y=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}ALR^{\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}$$
1 · Substitute the stated quantity
$$Y=(AL)^{1-\alpha}\left[\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}ALR^{\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}\right]^\alpha R^\beta$$
2 · Collect the powers
$$Y=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(AL)^{1-\alpha+\alpha}R^{\frac{\alpha\beta}{1-\alpha}+\beta}$$
3 · Simplify the resource exponent
$$\frac{\alpha\beta}{1-\alpha}+\beta=\frac{\alpha\beta+\beta(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}=\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}$$
$$Y=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}ALR^{\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}$$

Consistency correction. Profit is denoted by $\pi(x)$, to distinguish it from the input price $p(x)$.

Growth condition

When can innovation offset depletion?

With a constant labour allocation, growth combines technological progress and resource depletion:

$$g_Y=g_A-\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}q$$

Research effort

$L_R$: labour devoted to research.

Research efficiency

$\lambda$: how effectively research produces innovation.

Innovation size

$\gamma>1$: the technological improvement when research succeeds.

$$g_A=(\gamma-1)\lambda L_R$$
$$g_Y=(\gamma-1)\lambda L_R-\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}q$$
1 · Read growth from the output expression
$$g_Y=g_A+\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}g_R$$
2 · Insert resource decline and technological progress
$$g_R=-q,\qquad g_A=(\gamma-1)\lambda L_R$$
$$g_Y=(\gamma-1)\lambda L_R-\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}q$$

Positive growth requires enough research employment:

$$L_R>\frac{\beta q}{(1-\alpha)(\gamma-1)\lambda}$$
1 · Require gY > 0
$$(\gamma-1)\lambda L_R>\frac{\beta q}{1-\alpha}$$
2 · Divide by the positive research term
$$L_R>\frac{\beta q}{(1-\alpha)(\gamma-1)\lambda}$$
3 · Respect the labour constraint

Since $L_R<1$, that threshold must itself be below one:

$$\frac{\beta q}{(1-\alpha)(\gamma-1)\lambda}<1$$
What makes positive growth feasible?

More efficient research, larger innovations and/or slower depletion allow innovation to offset the shrinking resource flow.

The DICE model and climate policy

DICE

Linking the world economy and the climate

DICE — the Dynamic Integrated Climate–Economy model — extends Ramsey by linking the world economy and the climate.

1

Production

Economic activity generates greenhouse-gas emissions.

2

Climate change

Emissions affect the climate.

3

Economic damage

Climate change harms economic agents. This is a pollution externality.

Private production decisions

The competitive equilibrium does not fully account for the climate damage imposed on others.

The social planner’s perspective

A benevolent planner includes this damage when evaluating production and emissions. The comparison presented therefore associates the planner’s choice with less production and fewer emissions than the uncorrected competitive allocation.

The policy implication

The optimal carbon tax is positive: emissions carry a social cost that private decisions need to take into account.

The policy problem

Choose emissions control to maximise welfare over time, accounting for climate damage.

Objective

Discounted economic welfare

Objective

Maximise discounted economic welfare: consumption utility, population and the timing of welfare all matter.

$$\frac{c(t)^{1-\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}L(t)$$

$c(t)$

Consumption per person.

$L(t)$

Population and labour force.

$1/\epsilon$

Intertemporal elasticity of substitution.

Discounting

The weight assigned to future welfare relative to present welfare.

Pure time preference: ρ

$\rho$ reflects the weight placed on the timing of utility itself.

The precautionary term: pr

This term captures uncertainty about consumption growth. It reduces the discount rate in the specification described.

The climate-investment adjustment: ad

Climate investment involves risk that cannot be eliminated through diversification. The adjustment for that risk increases the discount rate.

The combined discount rate varies over time and declines through the precautionary term. Some policy scenarios add further constraints.

Production

Output after climate damage and abatement costs

$$Y_t=(1-\Lambda(t))(1-\Omega(t))A(t)K(t)^\alpha L(t)^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1$$

$\Omega(t)$ · Damage

The output loss caused by climate change.

$\Lambda(t)$ · Abatement

The resources used to reduce emissions.

Production before the two deductions

$A(t)K(t)^\alpha L(t)^{1-\alpha}$ combines productivity, capital and labour.

Climate damage

Multiplication by $1-\Omega(t)$ represents the part of production left after climate damage.

Abatement costs

Multiplication by $1-\Lambda(t)$ represents the resources used to control emissions. Reducing emissions is therefore associated with a cost in the production equation.

Damage function

Temperature change and output losses

$$\Omega(t)=\Phi_1T_{AT}(t)+\Phi_2T_{AT}(t)^2$$

$T_{AT}(t)$

Temperature change relative to the preindustrial level.

$\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$

Coefficients that translate warming into an output loss.

Reading the function

A temperature term and a squared-temperature term describe how damage responds to warming.

Abatement

The cost of controlling emissions

$$\Lambda(t)=\theta_1(t)\mu(t)^{\theta_2}$$

$\mu(t)$

The emissions-control rate: the degree of emissions reduction.

$\theta_1(t)$

The fraction of output needed to reduce emissions to zero.

$\theta_2$

The shape of the relationship between emissions control and its cost.

A backstop technology would allow emissions to be reduced to zero. Its price determines the cost of a zero-emissions policy.

Technological improvement

The model assumes that technologies for zero emissions improve over time, so the backstop cost falls.

Emissions

From production to greenhouse-gas emissions

Add production-related and land-related CO₂ emissions, then apply the emissions-control rate:

$$ECO_2(t)=\bigl(\sigma(t)Y(t)+ECO_{2,\mathrm{land}}(t)\bigr)(1-\mu(t))$$
Production-related emissions

$\sigma(t)$ is the emissions intensity multiplying output $Y(t)$. The product $\sigma(t)Y(t)$ is the production-related component.

$ECO_{2,\mathrm{land}}(t)$ adds emissions associated with land.

SCC

The social cost of carbon

Definition

The social cost of carbon (SCC) is the monetary value of the damage caused by one additional tonne of CO₂.

It is used in benefit–cost analysis. Estimating it combines socioeconomic projections, climate models, damage functions and discounting.

Socioeconomic projections

Probabilistic projections describe possible future economic and demographic conditions.

Climate models

Climate models relate emissions to changes in the climate.

Damage functions

Damage functions translate climate changes into consequences for society and the economy.

Discounting methods

Discounting determines the weight given to damage occurring at different future dates.

Model differences

Why do damage estimates differ so much?

1 · Which impacts are counted?

Temperature only, or also precipitation changes and more intense extreme weather? Coverage changes the estimated loss.

2 · How rapidly do losses increase?

Estimates differ on how damage intensifies with warming. Future adaptation, or its absence, remains a source of uncertainty.

Coverage concerns which climate effects are counted. Intensification concerns how strongly the losses from those effects increase as warming rises. These are two separate reasons why damage functions can deliver different estimates.

3 · Does the shock change output’s level or its growth rate?

Level effects

Permanent climate change affects output itself. Once the climate stabilises, the economy resumes growth at its pre-shock rate. The statement concerns the growth rate after stabilisation; it does not say that the output loss disappears.

Growth effects

A permanent climate shock changes the growth rate itself. Unless the climate returns to its pre-shock state, the country’s growth rate remains altered, usually negatively.

With level effects, the damage changes the output level while the previous growth rate can resume after climate stabilisation. With growth effects, the growth rate itself stays different. This distinction is why the two approaches imply very different loss estimates over long horizons.

Climate policy with directed technical change

Directed innovation

Innovation responds to economic incentives

Exogenous technology

DICE takes technological change as given. The policy response described is a Pigovian emissions tax that increases over time.

Endogenous technology

With directed technical change (DTC), incentives determine which technologies improve. Research responds to profitability.

The question is not only whether technology improves, but also which technology improves. Research may be directed towards saving energy or towards developing a cleaner substitute for a polluting input. Policy recommendations can change when these research choices become part of the model.

Complementary inputs

Energy or fossil-fuel resources are difficult to replace with capital or labour. Innovation is studied as a way of saving the energy or resource needed for production.

Substitutable inputs

Clean and dirty inputs can replace each other, as with renewable and fossil-fuel sources of electricity. The AABH model studies innovation towards the clean alternative.

The substitutable-input case produces the sharper change in policy recommendations.

AABH

Four policy messages to be explained

AABH: Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn and Hémous.

Act early

Intervention may need to be immediate and decisive.

Act temporarily

Support may only be needed for a limited period.

Limit growth costs

The long-run growth cost may be limited.

Combine instruments

Optimal regulation needs a tax and research subsidies.

The four messages address timing, duration, growth costs and policy instruments. Their mechanisms are developed in the model below.

These conclusions depend on the model’s conditions.

Production

Clean and dirty inputs

An infinite horizon in discrete time. Competitive final-good producers combine clean input $Y_c$ and dirty input $Y_d$:

$$Y=\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$
Key assumption

$\varepsilon>1$: clean and dirty inputs can substitute for each other. The elasticity measures how easily they do so.

Each sector $j=c,d$ combines labour $L_j$ with machines $x_{ji}$, indexed by $i$ from zero to one:

$$Y_j=L_j^\beta\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
The sector index j

$j=c$ identifies clean production and $j=d$ dirty production. The same input-sector technology is used for both.

The machine index i

$x_{ji}$ is the quantity of machine $i$ used in sector $j$, and $A_{ji}$ is its technological quality. The integral adds the contributions of the different machines.

Market structure

The final good and the two inputs are produced competitively. Machines are supplied by monopolists, using the final good as their production input.

Innovation

Scientists improve machine quality

Choose a sector

$h_j$.

Research choice

Where will scientists choose to innovate?

Scientists choose the sector with higher expected profit. Compare clean and dirty research:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

Ratio > 1

Research goes to the clean sector.

Ratio < 1

Research goes to the dirty sector.

Ratio = 1

Scientists may work in both sectors.

Research success probabilities

$h_c/h_d$ compares the probability of a successful clean innovation with that of a successful dirty innovation.

The price effect
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}$$

Relative sector prices enter the reward from innovation. Their direction is determined using the equilibrium price relationship below.

The market-size effect
$$\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}$$

The allocation of labour measures the relative scale of the two input sectors and the corresponding market for machines.

The direct productivity effect
$$\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

An innovation builds on inherited quality. This factor favours the sector that already has the higher technological level.

Equilibrium

Price and market size pull in different directions

Labour allocation is endogenous. Equalise the value of its marginal product across sectors:

$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}$$
Price effect → less advanced sector

The more advanced sector has the lower price, which works against its innovation advantage.

The relative technology level is raised to $-\beta$. A technological advantage is associated with a lower relative price. Through the price term in research profits, this works against the advantage of the more advanced sector.

Final-good demand gives the relative input quantities:

$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$

Combine this demand with sector output to obtain labour allocation:

$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1},\qquad\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)>1$$
1 · Take the ratio of sector outputs
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}\frac{L_c}{L_d}\frac{A_c}{A_d}$$
2 · Substitute the equilibrium relative price
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)}\frac{L_c}{L_d}\frac{A_c}{A_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}$$
3 · Use final-good demand
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon-\beta}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
Market-size effect → more advanced sector

Because $\sigma-1>0$, the more advanced sector attracts more labour.

Laissez-faire

Path dependence in the direction of innovation

Combine the profit ratio with prices, labour allocation and technology accumulation:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
1 · Combine price and market size
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1},\qquad\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
2 · Express current quality using inherited quality
$$\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}=\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
3 · Collect the inherited-technology powers

The exponents on the inherited technology ratio add up to $(\sigma-2)+1=\sigma-1$. This produces the expression displayed above.

Which effects dominate?

The direct productivity and market-size effects dominate the price effect. Innovation tends towards the sector already ahead.

Path dependence

An economy with a sufficiently large dirty-technology lead today tends to develop further dirty innovations. The inherited advantage shapes future research, reinforcing the direction already taken.

With an initial dirty lead, private research incentives do not automatically redirect innovation towards clean production.

Environment

Dirty production depletes environmental quality

Dirty production

Depletes environmental quality $S$.

Regeneration

Part of environmental quality recovers naturally.

$$S_{t+1}=-\chi Y_{dt}+(1+\delta)S_t$$
Damage from dirty production

The term $-\chi Y_{dt}$ is the depletion of environmental quality associated with dirty output. The coefficient $\chi$ measures that effect.

Environmental regeneration

The term $(1+\delta)S_t$ includes the existing stock and regeneration at rate $\delta>0$. Here $\delta$ means environmental regeneration, rather than capital depreciation.

Reference and boundary

$\bar S$ is preindustrial environmental quality. The model specifies an absorbing boundary at $S=0$: after reaching that boundary, environmental quality does not recover. The displayed transition is read together with that boundary condition.

Laissez-faire outcome

If fossil-fuel technologies start ahead, dirty output and CO₂ emissions grow without bound in the model. This depletes environmental quality.

Policy instruments

Redirecting innovation towards clean technologies

$q_t$ · Research subsidy

Raises the reward from clean research.

$\tau_t$ · Carbon tax

Changes the price incentive attached to dirty production.

Here $q_t$ denotes a subsidy, rather than the earlier depletion rate.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}={}&(1+q_t)(1+\tau_t)^\varepsilon\frac{h_c}{h_d}\\&\times\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The subsidy directly increases the reward from clean research. In the profit ratio, it appears as the multiplier $1+q_t$, making clean innovation more attractive relative to dirty innovation.

The tax creates a difference between the price paid for the dirty input and the price received by its producer. For given technologies, it lowers the dirty producer’s price. Its combined effect on the research comparison is represented by $(1+\tau_t)^\varepsilon$ in the displayed ratio.

Why intervention can be temporary

The aim is to change the technological lead that governs innovation incentives. The policy message is that support may only be needed long enough for clean technologies to establish that lead; temporary intervention can then suffice.

Timing

Why delaying intervention is costly

During catch-up

Research targets the initially less productive clean input. Productivity growth is low while the gap closes.

If intervention is delayed

Dirty technologies keep advancing. A larger gap later requires a longer, costlier intervention.

While policy is delayed

The economy remains on the laissez-faire path and the technological gap widens.

When policy begins

Research must be redirected towards a clean sector that is further behind.

During the transition

Closing the larger gap requires a longer intervention. The low-growth catch-up phase lasts longer, making the later intervention more costly.

Welfare

Consumption and environmental quality both matter

A representative household values consumption and environmental quality. With no population growth, the planner maximises:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{u(C_t,S_t)}{(1+\rho)^t}$$

$u(C_t,S_t)$

Utility from consumption and environmental quality at date $t$.

Discounting

$(1+\rho)^t$ reduces the weight on later utility.

Preferences

Utility is increasing and concave in both arguments: both are valued, with diminishing marginal utility.

$$\lim_{S\to0^+}u(C,S)=-\infty,\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial S}(C,\bar S)=0$$
As environmental quality approaches zero

Utility tends to minus infinity. Environmental collapse therefore has an extreme welfare cost in the model; environmental quality is critical to the household’s welfare.

At preindustrial environmental quality

The marginal utility of environmental quality is zero at $\bar S$. This condition concerns the additional utility from a marginal increase in environmental quality at that reference level.

First best

A carbon tax and clean-innovation subsidies

Decentralise the first best: use policies that make private choices implement the planner’s allocation.

Carbon tax

Addresses the pollution externality.

Clean-research subsidy

Addresses innovation incentives.

Subsidy to all machines

Removes the monopoly distortion.

The pollution externality

The carbon tax makes the emissions cost relevant to private production decisions.

Research externalities and the short horizon of profits

An innovator receives profit for only one period. Private research choices do not account for all the effects of innovation on subsequent research and technology. Clean-research subsidies address the innovation side of the policy problem.

The monopoly distortion in machines

A subsidy to machine use removes the distortion associated with monopoly supply. This is an additional part of the first-best policy package stated in the model.

The first-best result is stated without a full welfare proof.

Energy-saving innovation with complementary inputs

Energy-saving DTC

Improving energy services when substitution is difficult

A different route

Improve technologies that save energy or resources. Final output combines a production input $Y_P$ and energy services $Y_E$.

$$Y_t=\left(Y_{Pt}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_{Et}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$
Key assumption

$0<\varepsilon<1$: energy services are difficult to replace with the other input. The inputs are complementary.

$$Y_P=L^\beta\int_0^1 A_{Pi}^{\beta}x_{Pi}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
$$Y_E=E^\beta\int_0^1 A_{Ei}^{\beta}x_{Ei}^{1-\beta}\,di$$
The production input

$Y_P$ uses labour $L$ and its sector’s machines. Their quality is denoted by $A_{Pi}$.

Energy services

$Y_E$ uses the physical resource or energy flow $E$ and energy-sector machines. Their quality $A_{Ei}$ determines the technology with which that physical flow produces energy services.

The innovation question

Innovation can improve either input’s technology. The rest of the framework follows the research-incentive approach used in AABH.

Research incentives

Innovation favours the relatively lagging input

Compare expected profits from production-sector research and energy-sector research, using current technology levels:

$$\frac{\Pi_{Pt}}{\Pi_{Et}}=\frac{h_P}{h_E}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_Es_{Et}}{1+\gamma h_Ps_{Pt}}\right)\left(\frac{LA_{Pt}}{EA_{Et}}\right)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}$$
$$\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1),\qquad 0<\sigma<1$$
The two research opportunities

$h_P/h_E$ compares research success probabilities. $s_{Pt}$ and $s_{Et}$ are the fractions of scientists working in the two sectors.

Effective input quantities

$LA_{Pt}$ is labour multiplied by production technology, while $EA_{Et}$ is the physical energy flow multiplied by energy-service technology. The final ratio compares these effective quantities.

The role of complementarity

With $0<\sigma<1$, the exponent $(\sigma-1)/\sigma$ is negative. This is the part of the expression associated with innovation towards the relatively lagging input, rather than reinforcement of a technological lead.

Formula consistency note

Consistency correction. The slides contradict $\varepsilon<1$ by writing $\varepsilon>1$ and $\sigma>1$. The complementary-input case requires $0<\sigma<1$. The indices $c/d$ are replaced by $P/E$. Current technology levels are used in this displayed form to make the profit ratio consistent with the technology-accumulation equations; the lagged indices in the screenshot do not match its innovation multiplier.

The shale-gas revolution and the energy transition

Shale gas

Coal, gas and clean energy in one model

Acemoglu, Aghion, Barrage and Hémous study a gas boom in a model with coal, gas and clean energy. Innovation responds to profitability.

Final production combines the general input $Y_{Pt}$ and effective electricity $\widetilde A_tE_t$. Climate damage reduces output through $1-D(S_t)$:

$$Y_t=(1-D(S_t))\left((1-\nu)Y_{Pt}^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}+\nu(\widetilde A_tE_t)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}}$$
The general input and electricity

$Y_{Pt}$ is the general production input. $E_t$ is electricity, and $\widetilde A_t$ scales it into effective energy services. The weights $1-\nu$ and $\nu$ determine how the two terms enter the production function.

Limited substitution

$\lambda<1$ is the elasticity of substitution between the general input and electricity. These inputs are difficult to substitute for each other.

Climate damage

The factor $1-D(S_t)$ reduces output according to the damage function evaluated at the environmental state $S_t$.

Electricity can come from three substitutable sources:

$$E_t=\left(\kappa_cE_{ct}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+\kappa_gE_{gt}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+\kappa_sE_{st}^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$

$E_{ct}$ denotes coal-based electricity, $E_{gt}$ gas-based electricity and $E_{st}$ clean electricity. The coefficients $\kappa_c$, $\kappa_g$ and $\kappa_s$ are the weights on those sources. Here $\varepsilon>1$: the sources are substitutable in generating electricity, even though electricity and the general input are difficult to substitute in final production.

Formula consistency note

Consistency correction. The verbal description mentioning “coal, gas and oil” conflicts with the stated model and its three terms. The sources here are coal, gas and clean energy.

Results

Short-run emissions and the long-run transition

Short run: substitution away from coal

A natural-gas boom reduces carbon emissions in the short run by encouraging substitution from coal to gas. This result uses the lower CO₂ emissions of gas per unit of electricity.

Long run: weaker clean innovation

The gas boom discourages clean-energy innovation. It delays, and may even prevent, the transition to zero carbon.

The short-run result concerns the electricity mix: more gas replaces coal. The long-run result also takes account of where research goes. A cleaner mix relative to coal today can therefore coexist with weaker development of zero-carbon technologies over time.

Laissez-faire

The gas boom reduces social welfare in the model.

Appropriate policies

The same boom can increase welfare.

The welfare result is conditional on policy. The same gas boom is evaluated differently when policy responds to its effects on emissions and innovation. The supplied material states this comparison without specifying the full policy package or providing a numerical welfare calculation.
