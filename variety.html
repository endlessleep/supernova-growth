Chapter 3 — Innovation and Growth

Growth in a model with variety of intermediate goods

Introduction

Context

From the AK model to variety of goods

In the AK model, capital has diminishing marginal returns at the level of an individual firm: an additional unit of capital normally adds less to that firm’s output than the previous one. The model assumes externalities that exactly offset this effect at the aggregate level. The marginal return to aggregate capital is therefore constant, so growth does not become exhausted as it does in the neo-classical growth model.

The strong AK assumption

$$\eta+\alpha=1$$

The condition says that the two exponents add up to one. In the AK case, this is precisely the condition that keeps the aggregate return to capital constant.

This chapter uses a different source of long-run growth. R&D creates new intermediate-good varieties, so the final-good sector can produce with a wider set of inputs over time.

A model with several production sectors

The variety-of-goods model is more complex because it describes several production sectors at the same time. Each sector has its own input and output, and the output of one sector can become an input for another.

1

Intentional innovation

Technological change comes from deliberate investment decisions. Profit-maximizing agents devote resources to R&D because creating a new variety can generate a profit.

2

Technology as a special input

Technology is nonrival: once an idea exists, it can be used again without being used up. It is partially excludable, meaning that access to the idea can be restricted. It is therefore neither a conventional good, which one user consumes, nor a public good, which is freely available to all users.

3

Monopolistic competition

Creating a new technology requires an initial R&D effort. Once the idea exists, allowing one additional use does not require creating it again. The cost of creating the technology and the cost of an additional use are therefore different: this is the nonconvexity in the model. Price-taking competition cannot support this equilibrium, so each variety is supplied under monopolistic competition.

What the model concludes

Human capital determines the growth rate. In this model, the labour available for research is what creates new varieties. The equilibrium devotes too little human capital to research. Integration into world markets raises growth rates, whereas a large population by itself is not sufficient to generate growth.

Framework

Building blocks of the model

Model simplification

The model leaves out capital goods. It also treats the share of labour working in research as constant, so this share is an assumption of the simplified framework.

The economy has three production sectors. Their distinction matters because the sectors do not perform the same task: they use different inputs, produce different outputs and operate under different market structures.

1

Final-good sector

It combines labour $L_1$ with the intermediate goods already available to produce the final good $Y_t$. Under perfect competition, it takes the prices of labour and intermediate inputs as given.

2

Intermediate-good sector

It transforms units of the final good into intermediate inputs $x_i$. Each firm supplies one distinct variety and chooses the quantity of that variety under monopolistic competition.

3

Research sector

It uses research labour $L_2$ to create new varieties. Its output does not add units to an existing input: it raises $M_t$, the number of different inputs available to the final-good sector.

Household sector

The model represents households by one representative household, which chooses consumption over time by maximising an intertemporal utility function. Labour has no disutility in this utility function, and there is no population growth.

Links between the sectors

Research increases $M_t$, the number of varieties. Final-good firms use these varieties together with labour to make $Y_t$. Producing the intermediate inputs requires units of the final good, which is why this use must later be deducted when GDP is calculated.

Households and production

Households

Maximisation program

The household chooses a consumption path

Choice

Consumption over time

$c(t)$ is consumption at date $t$. The integral adds utility across all dates from today onwards. The term $e^{-\rho t}$ gives less weight to utility received later; $\rho$ is the discount rate and $\varepsilon$ is the parameter appearing in the utility expression.

Labour and prices

What the household takes as given

Labour $L$ is constant, so the household’s intertemporal choice concerns consumption. There is no population growth and labour does not enter utility as a cost. The household takes the wage $w(t)$ and the interest rate $r(t)$ as given.

Condition used on the infinite horizon

The transversality condition applies to discounted wealth. Along the path considered, wealth after discounting must approach zero as time becomes arbitrarily large.

$$\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)e^{-rt}=0$$
Result stated in the course

Once the household’s programme is solved, the growth rate of consumption is written as the difference between the interest rate and the discount rate, divided by $\varepsilon$:

$$g_C=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

Intermediate good sector

Production and GDP

Production cost

One-for-one requirement

Making one unit of an intermediate input requires one unit of the final good. Thus, producing $x_i$ units of variety $i$ uses $x_i$ units of final output.

$X_t$ is the total quantity of final good used to produce all intermediate varieties. The integral adds the quantity $x_i$ across the whole range of available varieties.

No capital goods

There is no investment term. The final good used to make intermediate inputs is already subtracted in $GDP_t=Y_t-X_t$, so the GDP that remains is consumed. Consumption and output therefore have the same growth rate.

Perfect competition

Price of intermediate input and wages

Perfect-competition rule

Final-good firms take prices as given. The price paid for labour or an intermediate input equals its marginal productivity: the additional final-good output obtained when that input is increased while the other inputs are held fixed.

Intermediate input $i$

Its price

$$p_i=L_1^{1-\alpha}\alpha x_i^{\alpha-1}$$

This expression is the marginal productivity of variety $i$. The final good is the numeraire, which means its price is chosen as 1; $p_i$ is therefore measured in units of the final good.

Labour

Its wage

$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$

This is the marginal productivity of labour in the final-good sector. The wage depends on the same integral that collects the contribution of all intermediate varieties.

Intermediate goods and variety

Monopolistic competition

Profit maximisation in the intermediate good sector

One firm supplies one varietyThe firm chooses the quantity $x_i$ of its own distinct input.
→
Its objectiveIt selects that quantity to maximise its profit $\Pi_i$.
→
Same choice for all firmsBecause varieties are treated symmetrically, each firm makes the same quantity choice.
Profit

Revenue minus production cost

$$\Pi_i=p_ix_i-x_i$$

The firm sells $x_i$ units at the price $p_i$, giving revenue $p_ix_i$. Producing those units requires $x_i$ units of final good, so its production cost is $x_i$.

After price substitution

The expression to maximise

$$\Pi_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha-x_i$$

The price formula from the final-good sector is inserted into the revenue term $p_ix_i$. Profit can then be written only in terms of the quantity the firm chooses, $x_i$.

Quantity selected by each intermediate-good firm
$$x_i=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$
1 · Substitute the price equation

From $p_i=L_1^{1-\alpha}\alpha x_i^{\alpha-1}$, revenue is $p_ix_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha$. Therefore $\Pi_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha-x_i$.

2 · Maximise with respect to $x_i$
$$\frac{d\Pi_i}{dx_i}=\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^{\alpha-1}-1=0$$

The first-order condition sets the marginal change in profit equal to zero.

3 · Isolate $x_i$
$$\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^{\alpha-1}=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_i^{1-\alpha}=\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}$$
$$x_i=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

Symmetry across varieties

The profit expression does not depend on the label $i$. No variety is treated differently in the model, so every firm produces the same quantity. Total intermediate production $X_t$ is consequently divided equally across the $M_t$ varieties.

$$\Pi_i=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$
$$x_i=\frac{X_t}{M_t}=x$$
Variety

Economic growth and the variety of intermediate inputs

Starting point

Every variety is used in the same quantity: $x_i=X_t/M_t$. In other words, total intermediate production $X_t$ is shared equally between the $M_t$ available varieties.

Production function$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$
→
Use $x_i=X_t/M_t$Each variety has the same quantity, so the term inside the integral is identical for every $i$.
→
Collect the $M_t$ varietiesAdding the identical contribution of all varieties introduces a factor $M_t$.
Output written with total intermediate production
$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t^{1-\alpha}X_t^\alpha$$
After substitution

It makes the integrand constant

After replacing $x_i$ by $X_t/M_t$, the integrand no longer varies with $i$. Integrating the same term from $0$ to $M_t$ therefore multiplies that term by the length of the interval, $M_t$.

Effect on output

More variety raises output

The rewritten production function contains $M_t^{1-\alpha}$. It shows that, for a given total quantity $X_t$, output increases when the economy has more varieties of intermediate inputs.

1 · Integrate the common quantity
$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t\left(\frac{X_t}{M_t}\right)^\alpha=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t^{1-\alpha}X_t^\alpha$$
2 · Use $X_t=M_tx$
$$Y_t=M_t^{1-\alpha}L_1^{1-\alpha}(M_tx)^\alpha=M_tL_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha$$
3 · Obtain GDP

Because total intermediate-good production is $X_t=M_tx$, subtracting it from $Y_t$ gives:

$$GDP_t=M_t\left(L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x\right)$$
Growth and variety

The common factor $M_t$ appears in both output and GDP. Their growth is therefore tied to the growth of the number of varieties. Since $C_t=GDP_t$, consumption follows the same growth rate as $Y_t$, $GDP_t$ and $M_t$.

Research sector

New varieties of intermediate goods

Research equation
$$\frac{dM_t}{dt}=\lambda M_tL_2$$
Existing variety

$M_t$

This is the stock of varieties already available at date $t$. It appears in the equation because the model lets existing varieties contribute to the creation of new ones.

Research labour

$L_2$

This is the amount of labour assigned to R&D rather than to final-good production. More research labour enters directly into the innovation flow.

Productivity

$\lambda$

$\lambda$ is the productivity of research. It converts the combination of existing varieties and research labour into the flow of new varieties.

The flow of innovation

$dM_t/dt$ is the number of new varieties created per unit of time. The equation says that this flow depends on the varieties already available, $M_t$, and on research labour, $L_2$. These new varieties then become inputs available to the final-good sector; this is the positive externality from research noted in the course.

Research sector

Profit in the research sector

One new varietygives its intermediate-good firm a profit flow $\Pi$ each period.
→
Value of the innovationWith the constant interest rate used here, that stream has value $\Pi/r$.
→
Research compares value and wage costThe value created by innovations is compared with the wage cost of $L_2$.
Profit flow

$\Pi$ from one variety

$$\Pi=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

This is the profit generated by one intermediate-good variety per unit of time. It is the profit result obtained in the intermediate-good sector.

Innovation value

Discounted profits

$$\frac{\Pi}{r}$$

An innovation produces the flow $\Pi$ over time. Using the constant profit flow and interest rate in the course, its value is written as the discounted value $\Pi/r$.

Research-sector profit
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\frac{dM_t}{dt}-w_tL_2$$
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2$$
First term

The value generated by the flow of innovations, $dM_t/dt$. Each new variety is valued at $\Pi/r$.

Second term

The wage cost of research labour: the wage $w_t$ paid to each of the $L_2$ research workers.

Free-entry condition

The research sector is perfectly competitive, so its equilibrium profit is zero. The value obtained from creating new varieties must then exactly equal the wage cost of research. Rearranging that equality gives:

$$r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t$$
1 · Substitute the innovation flow
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\frac{dM_t}{dt}-w_tL_2=\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2$$
2 · Apply zero profit
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2=0$$

For research labour $L_2$, divide by $L_2$ and rearrange:

$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_t=w_t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r=\frac{\lambda M_t\Pi}{w_t}$$
Interest rate

Interest rate

Using the earlier results

The research-arbitrage condition contains the value of an innovation and the research wage. The profit expression supplies the innovation value, while the final-good sector supplies the wage. Substituting both puts the condition in terms of the model’s basic variables.

All quantities are equalSymmetry gives $x_i=X_t/M_t=x$ for every variety.
→
Substitute the firm choiceThe common quantity is $x=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$.
→
Rewrite the wageThe wage can then be written only with $M_t$, $\alpha$ and the constants of the model.
Wage expression
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$

All varieties have the same quantity $x$, so each term inside the wage integral is the same. The integral therefore adds this common contribution over all $M_t$ varieties.

After substitution
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$$

Replacing the common quantity by the firm’s optimal choice removes $L_1$ from the wage expression. This wage and the profit flow $\Pi$ are then used in the research-arbitrage condition.

Interest-rate result
$$r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t=\alpha\lambda L_1$$

After the substitutions, the factors involving $M_t$ cancel. The interest rate is therefore determined by research productivity $\lambda$, the labour used in final production $L_1$, and $\alpha$.

1 · Substitute the common intermediate quantity into the wage
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}M_tx^\alpha$$
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}M_t\left(L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}\right)^\alpha=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$$
2 · Substitute profit and wage into the arbitrage condition
$$r=\lambda M_t\frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t}$$

The factors $M_t$ and $1-\alpha$ cancel. Combining the remaining powers of $\alpha$ gives:

$$r=\alpha\lambda L_1$$
Growth rate

The growth rate of the economy

The final calculation

The research equation, the household consumption-growth rule and the interest-rate result must hold together in equilibrium. Since GDP and consumption grow at the same rate in this model, these three relations can be combined to determine one common growth rate.

Research

Growth from new varieties

$$g=\lambda L_2$$

Dividing the research equation by $M_t$ turns the flow of new varieties into the growth rate of the variety stock. The result is $g=\lambda L_2$.

Household

Consumption growth

$$g_C=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

This is the consumption-growth result from the household programme. It must equal the common growth rate because consumption and GDP grow together.

Research arbitrage

Interest rate

$$r=\alpha\lambda L_1$$

This is the interest rate obtained after substituting the wage and profit expressions into the research-arbitrage condition.

Labour-market equilibrium

The fixed total quantity of labour $L$ is allocated between the two activities. Labour used for research is therefore not used in final-good production, and vice versa.

$$L_1+L_2=L$$
Equilibrium growth rate
$$\boxed{g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}}$$
1 · Equate GDP and consumption growth
$$g=g_C=\frac{\alpha\lambda L_1-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
2 · Use research growth and labour-market equilibrium
$$g=\lambda L_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_2=\frac{g}{\lambda}$$
$$L_1=L-L_2=L-\frac{g}{\lambda}$$
3 · Substitute $L_1$
$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda\left(L-\frac{g}{\lambda}\right)-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
$$\varepsilon g=\alpha\lambda L-\alpha g-\rho\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (\alpha+\varepsilon)g=\alpha\lambda L-\rho$$
$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}$$
Scale effect

Reading the formula

The growth formula increases with research productivity $\lambda$. It also increases with total labour $L$ when research is productive, because some of that labour can be assigned to R&D.

Limit stated in the course

When $\lambda=0$

If research productivity is zero, assigning more labour does not create new varieties through this mechanism. The course discusses the scale effect at country and world level when goods and patented ideas circulate.

Main conclusions

Summing up

The chapter in one mechanism

Research raises the number of varieties $M_t$. Those varieties enter the final-good production function, so the growth of output, GDP and consumption follows the growth of $M_t$.

1

Growth comes from variety

R&D raises $M_t$, the number of different intermediate inputs. The production side of the model then links this larger set of inputs to output, GDP and consumption.

2

Long-run growth is possible

The course attributes continuing growth to the constant marginal return of the variety of intermediate input. Growth is sustained through the expansion of varieties.

3

Trade raises growth

Integration gives intermediate-good firms access to wider markets. The resulting higher profits raise the incentive for R&D and can increase growth.

4

No convergence

The model does not predict that economies automatically move towards the same income level. This applies to both absolute and conditional convergence.

5

Market allocation is not optimal

The decentralized market allocation differs from the optimal allocation. The course links this result to imperfect competition in intermediate goods.

6

Policy can act

Tax policy can be used to bring the decentralized allocation closer to the optimal allocation described in the course.

Openness to trade and growth: model prediction

3. Economic integration and trade
Starting model

Labour is the only input used in R&D. The exercise starts from the equilibrium growth rate already obtained for the closed economy.

$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}$$
Countries

The two countries are identical and each has the same fixed labour endowment, written $L=L^*$.

Notation

A star marks the country initially treated as foreign. Thus, $L^*$ is the foreign country’s labour endowment.

a.

If the two countries merge and become a unique economy, what will be its growth rate of output?

Before integration, each country has labour endowment $L$ and the course gives $g=(\alpha\lambda L-\rho)/(\alpha+\varepsilon)$. After a merger, the unique economy has labour endowment $2L$.

$$\boxed{g_{\mathrm{merge}}=\frac{2\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}}$$

This applies the closed-economy result already established in the course to the combined labour force.

Trade scenario

At time zero, the two countries have the same number of varieties: $M_0=M_0^*$. Before trade, their sets of intermediate goods are disjoint, so a variety produced in one country is different from a variety produced in the other. Once trade starts, each monopolist can sell its own variety in both markets. The production function still depends only on domestic varieties.

b.

Compute the equilibrium profits and wages after trade is introduced.

In the closed economy, one variety produces $x$ for one market and earns $\Pi$. The statement says that, after trade, each monopolist sells its product in two markets. Its flow of profit is therefore the sum of the two identical market profits:

$$\boxed{\Pi^{\mathrm{trade}}=2\Pi}$$

The exercise also assumes that varieties used in final production depend only on domestic varieties. The supplied material does not provide enough information to reconstruct a different wage equation reliably; the wage formula already established remains $w_t=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$ for each domestic economy.

c.

Write down the research arbitrage condition after trade is introduced. Compare it to the one in the closed economy case. Discuss the result.

The closed-economy free-entry condition is $r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t$. With two-market profit $2\Pi$, the corresponding research-arbitrage condition is:

$$\boxed{r=\frac{\lambda M_t(2\Pi)}{w_t}}$$

Relative to the closed economy, trade changes the innovation value through the profit flow earned in two markets. This is the channel stated in the exercise and in the course summary: higher profits raise the incentive for R&D.

$$M_0<M_0^*.$$

It gives the domestic research equation $dM_t/dt=\lambda M_tL_2$ and the condition that trade equalizes wages and interest rates, but it does not provide the additional allocation equations needed to determine the home innovation flow.

The supplied course material does not contain enough information to reconstruct the foreign-country condition and long-run adjustment reliably. The exercise states the required comparison but does not provide the additional transition equations.

Exercise

Social Optimum in the model

5. Social optimum in the model

The change in perspective

The exercise moves from the market equilibrium to the allocation selected by a social planner over one period. The planner chooses intermediate-input use by looking directly at GDP rather than at the profit of one intermediate-good firm.

$$GDP_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di-\int_0^{M_t}x_i$$
a.

Solve for the social planner’s allocation of intermediate input. Compare it to the market equilibrium level. Explain your results.

With identical varieties, the planner’s objective can be written as $GDP_t=M_t[L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x]$. For a given $M_t$, the planner chooses $x$ to maximize the term in brackets.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x\right)=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}-1=0$$
$$x^{1-\alpha}=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x^{\mathrm{SP}}=L_1\alpha^{1/(1-\alpha)}}$$

The market equilibrium was $x^{\mathrm{market}}=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$. Since $0<\alpha<1$, the planner allocation is higher. This comparison uses the chapter’s imperfect-competition mechanism: the planner directly maximizes GDP, while the market quantity came from monopolistic firms maximizing their own profit.

b.

What is the social planner’s allocation of labor to R&D activities?

The supplied course material does not contain enough information to reconstruct this correction reliably. It gives a one-period planner objective over intermediate inputs, but does not provide the planner’s dynamic problem or the condition needed to choose $L_2$.

c.

Compare the efficient growth rate of the economy given by the social planner’s maximization problem to the market equilibrium growth rate. Interpret your findings.

The supplied material does not give enough equations to derive an efficient growth-rate formula. It does state the conclusion used for the comparison: the centralised economy achieves a higher growth rate than the decentralized economy, because the decentralized economy is not optimal under imperfect competition.

Economic policy

Economic Policy in an economy with variety of goods

Policy objective

The centralised economy has a higher growth rate than the decentralized economy. Policy is used to reproduce that allocation in the market economy, where imperfect competition makes the market outcome non-optimal.

Route 1

Subsidize intermediate goods

  • The subsidy lowers the price effectively paid by purchasers of intermediate goods until it equals the production cost.
  • Intermediate goods are then used at the production-cost allocation identified in the course.
  • A lump-sum tax on households finances the subsidy without changing the consumption-saving arbitrage.
Route 2

Subsidize final goods

  • The policy instead subsidizes final-good production.
  • Demand for intermediate goods then corresponds to demand at the production-cost price.
  • A lump-sum tax on households finances this subsidy as well.
