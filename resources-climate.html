Chapter 5 — Economic Growth

Growth, Natural Resources and Climate Change

Land and natural resources in a Solow model

Introduction

Growth with finite resources and climate change

Finite resource

Production may use an exhaustible natural resource. Its remaining stock falls as it is extracted.

Climate channel

Economic activity can affect the climate, and climate damage can then affect the economy.

The chapter question

How do these constraints change the possibilities for economic growth and the role of public policy?

Starting point

The Limits to Growth: the Meadows report

The Meadows report asks whether continuing economic and population growth is compatible with a finite supply of resources. Exponential growth means repeated proportional increases, while the resource stock available to support production is limited.

Its warning is conditional: without major changes in resource use and environmental destruction, population and industrial capacity may decline abruptly. The issue is therefore whether the way the economy uses resources can change enough to sustain growth.

DHSS

Can consumption be maintained despite depletion?

The Dasgupta–Heal–Solow–Stiglitz (DHSS) model responds to this concern. It studies the technological conditions under which consumption per person can be maintained even though the use of non-renewable resources must eventually decline.

Three possible ways to compensate

Input substitution allows production to replace some of the scarce resource with other inputs. How far firms can change this combination depends on the production technology.

Resource-augmenting technical progress makes each unit of the resource more productive. A smaller physical quantity can then provide more productive services than before.

Increasing returns to scale mean that production can rise more than proportionally when the scale of activity increases. This is the third mechanism identified in the course.

Production

Adding land and an exhaustible resource

Nordhaus adds fixed land and an exhaustible resource to the Solow production function:

$$Y=K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}$$

Goal: rewrite the same production function so that products become sums. All inputs are positive where we take logarithms; the exponents are constants.

1 · Take the natural logarithm of both sides
$$\ln Y=\ln\!\left[K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right]$$

$\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm. At this stage, we only apply the same operation to both sides.

2 · Turn the product into a sum

Use $\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v$ on the four multiplied factors:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\ln(K^\alpha)+\ln(T^\beta)\\&+\ln(R^\gamma)+\ln\!\left[(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right].\end{aligned}$$
3 · Bring each power in front of its logarithm

Use $\ln(u^a)=a\ln u$. For example, $\ln(K^\alpha)=\alpha\ln K$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\ln(AL).\end{aligned}$$
4 · Split effective labour into A and L
$$\ln(AL)=\ln A+\ln L$$
$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(\ln A+\ln L).\end{aligned}$$

Every term comes from one factor of the original function. We have rewritten output; we have not differentiated yet.

$Y$, $K$, $L$

Output, capital and labour.

$A$ and $AL$

Labour-augmenting productivity and effective labour.

$T$

Land: its quantity is fixed.

$S$ and $R$

The remaining resource stock and the extraction flow used in production.

The resource stock and the resource flow

$S$ is what remains available. $R$ is what is used per unit of time. Production depends on the flow $R$, while extraction changes the stock $S$. Keeping these two quantities distinct is essential for reading the model.

The production exponents

The exponents $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ determine how capital, land and resource use enter the Cobb–Douglas production function. The remaining exponent, $1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$, applies to effective labour.

$$\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=g_A,\qquad \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}=n,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}=0$$

Productivity

$A$ grows at the given rate $g_A$.

Population

$L$ grows at rate $n$.

Land

$T$ does not grow.

Resource use

A constant proportional extraction rate

$$\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS,\qquad \frac{1}{S}\frac{dS}{dt}=-q$$

Assumption

The extraction rate $q$ is constant: each period uses the same fraction of the remaining stock.

Mechanism

A smaller stock means a smaller extraction flow, even with the same proportional rate.

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Start from the extraction rule $R=qS$, with $q$ constant.

1 · Differentiate the extraction rule
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=q\frac{dS}{dt}=q(-qS)$$
2 · Use R = qS
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=-qR\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Both the stock and the extraction flow decline at the same proportional rate.

Fixing $q$ simplifies the model. Choosing resource use optimally across dates is the problem introduced by Hotelling.

Growth

The economy’s regular growth rate

Each input contributes its growth rate, weighted by its production exponent. Land does not grow; resource use grows at $-q$.

$$g_Y=\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
1 · Why differentiate a logarithm?

Each variable depends on time. The chain rule gives the derivative of $\ln X(t)$:

$$\frac{d\ln X(t)}{dt}=\frac{1}{X(t)}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}\equiv g_X.$$

$dX/dt$ is the change in the level. Dividing by $X$ makes it a proportional growth rate.

2 · Differentiate the logged production function

The exponents are constant, so they remain in front of the derivatives. Apply the same chain rule to every logarithm:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt}={}&\alpha\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}+\beta\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}\\&+\gamma\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\left(\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}+\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}\right).\end{aligned}$$
3 · Replace each proportional derivative by its name
$$g_Y=\alpha g_K+\beta g_T+\gamma g_R+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+g_L)$$

This is why each input’s growth rate is multiplied by its production exponent.

4 · Substitute the model’s assumptions
$$g_T=0,\qquad g_R=-q,\qquad g_L=n$$
$$\begin{aligned}g_Y={}&\alpha g_K+\beta(0)+\gamma(-q)\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n).\end{aligned}$$

Fixed land removes the $\beta g_T$ term. Declining resource use produces the negative term $-\gamma q$.

With a constant saving rate $s$ and capital depreciation $\delta$:

$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
1 · Divide every term of accumulation by K
$$\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=\frac{sY}{K}-\frac{\delta K}{K}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$

The left side is $g_K$. Capital cancels in the depreciation term.

2 · Isolate the output–capital ratio
$$\frac{Y}{K}=\frac{g_K+\delta}{s}$$

On a regular growth path, $g_K$ is constant. With constant $s>0$ and $\delta$, the right side is constant, so $Y/K$ is constant.

3 · Differentiate the logarithm of that constant ratio
$$\ln(Y/K)=\ln Y-\ln K$$
$$0=\frac{d\ln(Y/K)}{dt}=g_Y-g_K$$
$$g_Y=g_K$$

The zero comes from differentiating a constant. Output and capital must therefore grow at the same proportional rate.

1

Regular growth

Capital grows at a constant rate.

2

Constant output–capital ratio

$Y/K$ stays constant.

3

Same growth rate

Output and capital grow together: $g_Y=g_K$.

$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Substitute gK = gY
$$g_Y=\alpha g_Y-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
2 · Subtract αgY from both sides
$$g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$
$$(1-\alpha)g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$

Factor out $g_Y$ on the left: $g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha)g_Y$.

3 · Divide both sides by 1 − α
$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
4 · Read the condition for positive growth

Since $0<\alpha<1$, the denominator is positive. The fraction is positive exactly when its numerator is positive:

$$\begin{aligned}g_Y>0\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q>0\\&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q.\end{aligned}$$
Positive growth

The contribution of effective labour must exceed the loss from resource depletion.

$$(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q$$

If depletion is too fast, the condition fails.

Living standards

Growth of GDP per person

From total GDP to GDP per person

Subtract population growth: $g_Y-n$.

$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · GDP per person is Y divided by L
$$\ln(Y/L)=\ln Y-\ln L$$
$$\frac{d\ln(Y/L)}{dt}=g_Y-g_L=g_Y-n$$

The subtraction comes from the quotient $Y/L$. It is the growth rate of that quotient, not a subtraction from the level of output.

2 · Give both terms the same denominator
$$n=\frac{n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q-n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
3 · Expand and collect only the terms in n
$$\begin{aligned}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)n-n(1-\alpha)\\&\quad=n-\alpha n-\beta n-\gamma n-n+\alpha n\\&\quad=-\beta n-\gamma n=-(\beta+\gamma)n.\end{aligned}$$

The $n$ terms cancel, and so do the $\alpha n$ terms. The terms involving $g_A$ and $q$ are unchanged.

4 · Put the remaining terms together
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$

This is the per-capita growth formula shown above.

Condition to retain

Productivity must grow fast enough to offset population growth and the declining resource flow.

Growth drag

The drag on economic growth

The growth drag is the reduction in per-capita growth caused by the resource constraints.

Comparison path

Land and resource use grow with population.

Constrained path

Land is fixed and resource use declines.

$$\mathrm{drag}=\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Write growth on the comparison path

In this comparison, land and resource use both grow with population: $g_T=g_R=n$. Use the same production function and regular-growth condition $g_K=g_Y$:

$$\begin{aligned}(1-\alpha)g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}={}&\beta n+\gamma n\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A+(1-\alpha)n.\end{aligned}$$
2 · Convert the comparison to per-capita growth
$$g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}$$

Divide the previous line by $1-\alpha$, then subtract $n$. Its population term cancels.

3 · Subtract constrained growth from comparison growth
$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{drag}={}&\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}\\&-\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}\\={}&\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}.\end{aligned}$$

The productivity terms cancel. The minus sign before the second fraction turns its two negative terms into positive terms.

Reading the sign

The drag is a positive gap: it measures how much the constraints reduce growth.

Substitution

Why the elasticity of substitution matters

The Cobb–Douglas production function has an elasticity of substitution equal to one. It allows firms to compensate for a scarcer resource by changing their combination of inputs. If this elasticity is below one, replacing the resource becomes more difficult.

As the resource becomes scarcer, a growing share of income goes to it. That share tends towards one, while the shares received by the other inputs become small. The constraint imposed by the resource therefore becomes increasingly important for growth.

In the limiting outcome described in the course, the growth drag approaches $n+q$, and GDP per person falls at the rate $n+q$.

Hotelling

Choosing resource use across time

Starting point

The resource’s income share is constant, while the quantity used falls. The resource price rises.

What is missing?

Fixing extraction $q$ and the saving rate does not establish an optimal allocation.

Hotelling’s problem: choose consumption and resource use together, accounting for capital accumulation and depletion.

$$\max\int_0^\infty\frac{C(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=Y-C-\delta K,\qquad \frac{dS}{dt}=-R$$
$$Y=K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}$$

Consumption and discounting

$C$ is consumption. Its utility is added across dates, with $e^{-\rho t}$ giving less weight to later utility. The parameter $\rho$ is the rate of time preference; $\varepsilon$ determines the curvature of consumption utility.

Capital accumulation

Output left after consumption and depreciation adds to capital. The first constraint records that allocation.

Resource depletion

Using $R$ units of the resource reduces the remaining stock by the same amount. The production function in this problem includes the resource flow and no separate land term.

Hotelling exercise

Two controls, two states, four conditions

CONTROL VARIABLES · choices

$C$ = consumption.
$R$ = resource use.
Choose their values at each date.

STATE VARIABLES · stocks

$K$ = capital.
$S$ = resource left.
They change through accumulation and extraction.

The current-value Hamiltonian adds current utility and the value of changes in both stocks:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal H={}&\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\\&+\lambda_K\left[K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}-C-\delta K\right]\\&-\lambda_SR.\end{aligned}$$

Capital

$\lambda_K$: the implicit price of one more unit of capital.

Resource

$\lambda_S$: the implicit price of one more unit of the resource left in the ground.

An implicit price measures how much an extra unit of a stock is worth in this optimisation problem.

A · FOCs for CONTROL VARIABLES: C and R

For each control, set its partial derivative to zero: $\partial\mathcal H/\partial C=0$ and $\partial\mathcal H/\partial R=0$.

1 · Consumption C
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial C}=C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0$$
Meaning

The gain from consuming one more unit equals the value of keeping that unit as capital: $C^{-\varepsilon}=\lambda_K$.

Differentiate the two terms containing C
$$\frac{d}{dC}\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{\partial(-\lambda_K C)}{\partial C}=-\lambda_K$$
Set the derivative to zero
$$C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_K=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
2 · Resource use R
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S=0$$
Meaning

Using one more unit of the resource creates output worth $\lambda_K\gamma Y/R$, but removes a unit from the stock worth $\lambda_S$. At the optimum, gain = cost.

Differentiate production
$$\frac{\partial Y}{\partial R}=\gamma K^\alpha R^{\gamma-1}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}=\gamma\frac{Y}{R}$$
Include the loss from extraction
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S$$
$$\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}=\lambda_S$$

B · Conditions for STATE VARIABLES: K and S

Use the costate equations for their implicit prices. Do not set the stock derivatives of $\mathcal H$ to zero.

3 · Capital stock K
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K$$
Meaning

The value of capital changes with impatience $\rho$, depreciation $\delta$ and its marginal product $\alpha Y/K$.

Differentiate the Hamiltonian with respect to K
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}=\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)$$
Use the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\rho\lambda_K-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}&=\rho\lambda_K-\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)\\&=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K.\end{aligned}$$
4 · Resource stock S
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Meaning

The Hamiltonian contains the extraction flow $R$, but no direct term in the remaining stock $S$. Therefore $\partial\mathcal H/\partial S=0$.

Apply the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}$$
Use the absence of a direct S term
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Divide by the implicit price of the resource to obtain its growth rate
$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$
Result to remember

The resource’s current-value implicit price grows at the rate of time preference $\rho$.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$

Economic meaning

Keeping a unit in the ground allows it to be used later. Its future implicit price must compensate for the delay in utility.

Why the stock still matters

Extraction removes future resources. This cost is recorded by $-\lambda_SR$, even though $S$ does not appear directly in the Hamiltonian.

Which price?

This result concerns $\lambda_S$, the implicit price of the resource in this exercise.

Historical discussion

Wood, coal and the timing of resource use

The transition from wood to coal can be understood in two ways. They differ in which event causes the other.

Scarcity encourages innovation

In the first interpretation, deforestation exhausts wood and creates a need for another energy source. Coal combustion then provides an alternative. Depletion comes before the search for a replacement.

An alternative changes the incentive to conserve

In the interpretation associated with Dasgupta and Stiglitz, coal use is already known but initially costs more than wood. Once it becomes an available alternative, energy supply depends less on scarce wood.

The incentive to preserve forests weakens, so deforestation can occur. Here, access to the alternative helps explain depletion.

Discussion: electricity storage and oil

If the second interpretation applies to electricity storage and oil, what could happen once storage becomes available? Is this outcome likely, and what could prevent the problem? These remain open questions.

Natural resources in endogenous growth models

AK model

Why the resource constraint ends permanent AK growth

Add the resource flow $R$ to AK production, with $0<\gamma<1$ and a constant saving rate $s$:

$$Y=AKR^\gamma$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS$$
$$g_K=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
1 · Divide accumulation by capital
$$g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
2 · Insert resource-dependent production
$$g_K=s\frac{AKR^\gamma}{K}-\delta=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
3 · Read the effect of declining resource use

As $R$ falls, $R^\gamma$ becomes smaller. The positive accumulation term shrinks, while depreciation remains. Eventually $g_K$ becomes negative and tends towards $-\delta$.

Result

The declining resource term eventually outweighs accumulation. Permanent AK growth disappears; capital and production contract.

The DICE model and climate policy

DICE

Linking the world economy and the climate

The Dynamic Integrated Climate–Economy model (DICE) adds the two directions missing from a standard growth model: economic activity affects the climate, and climate change affects the economy. Production creates emissions; emissions affect the climate; and climate damage reduces the output available to the economy.

STARTProductioncreates emissions
THENEmissionschange the climate
FINALLYClimate damagereduces available output

Pollution externality

Production emits greenhouse gases and contributes to climate change, which harms all agents. Producers do not bear all of this future damage, so the competitive decentralised equilibrium is not socially optimal.

Planner and policy

A social planner accounts for both the current cost of abatement and the future damage it avoids when choosing growth and emissions control. A carbon tax gives firms a reason to take that social damage into account.

Objective

Discounted economic welfare

The planner compares welfare at different dates. The objective first values consumption for each person, then aggregates it over the population and through time.

THE PLANNER ADDS WELFARE ACROSS TIME
$$W=\int_{2020}\frac{c(t)^{1-\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}L(t)e^{-(\rho+pr+ad)}$$
Consumption utility

$c(t)^{1-\epsilon}/(1-\epsilon)$: welfare per person. $1/\epsilon$ describes willingness to shift consumption between dates.

Population

$L(t)$: turns per-person welfare into total welfare.

Discounting

$e^{-(\rho+pr+ad)}$: gives a weight to future welfare.

Integral

Adds weighted welfare over time.

Why future welfare receives a different weight

The exponential term is multiplied by utility because the planner does not give the same weight to welfare at every date. Its discount rate has three components, each with a separate role.

Pure time preference: $\rho$

$\rho$ captures a preference for utility sooner rather than later. It therefore raises the discount rate applied to future welfare.

Precautionary term: $pr$

$pr$ reflects uncertainty about consumption growth. In this objective, it reduces the discount rate.

Climate-investment adjustment: $ad$

$ad$ adjusts for the non-diversifiable risk of climate investment. In this objective, it increases the discount rate.

Discounting through time

The three components make the discount rate vary over time. The precautionary term is the force that makes the rate decline over time in this framework.

This objective does not yet tell us what the economy can consume. The next equation determines the output available after climate damage and abatement costs.

Production

$A(t)K(t)^\alpha L(t)^{1-\alpha}$ is output before climate considerations. $1-\Omega(t)$ is the share remaining after climate damage, while $1-\Lambda(t)$ is the share remaining after the resources used for abatement have been deducted. Both apply to the same production base.

Temperature change and output losses

The damage term is built from atmospheric temperature change. The linear term links damage directly to warming; the squared term allows the relationship to change as warming becomes larger.

The coefficients $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ are chosen so that a given temperature change corresponds to a calibrated loss of GDP.

Abatement

The cost of controlling emissions

MORE CONTROL USES MORE RESOURCES
$$\Lambda(t)=\theta_1(t)\mu(t)^{\theta_2}$$

The planner controls emissions through the abatement rate $\mu(t)$. This is not free: cleaner production or avoided emissions use output that cannot be used elsewhere. $\Lambda(t)$ is this resource cost in the production equation above.

Control rate: $\mu(t)$

$\mu(t)$ is the fraction of emissions controlled. When $\mu(t)=1$, the model describes full emissions control.

Cost at full control: $\theta_1(t)$

$\theta_1(t)$ is the fraction of output required to reduce emissions to zero at date $t$. Indeed, when $\mu(t)=1$, the formula gives $\Lambda(t)=\theta_1(t)$.

How costs change: $\theta_2$

$\theta_2$ determines how the abatement cost changes as the control rate becomes more stringent.

Backstop technology

A backstop technology makes zero emissions possible. Its price determines the cost of a zero-emission policy, and improvements in this technology lower that cost over time.

What the calibration captures

$\theta_1(t)$ and $\theta_2$ are calibrated to represent both the cost of reaching zero emissions and technological improvement in the means of doing so.

Production emissions and land-use emissions have different origins, so the model first adds them. It then applies $1-\mu(t)$, the fraction of total emissions that remains after abatement.

Every scenario uses the same link from output to emissions, climate damage and welfare. What changes is the rule for choosing $\mu(t)$ or the assumptions used to evaluate that choice, so each simulation answers a distinct policy question.

Model differences

Why do damage estimates differ so much?

An SCC is not a single mechanical number. It changes with the climate effects included, the way warming becomes damage, the role of adaptation and the weight attached to future losses.

Effects included

Some damage functions use temperature only; others also include precipitation changes and more frequent extreme weather.

Damage profile

Models can disagree about how quickly losses intensify as warming rises. This is especially uncertain when future adaptation is difficult to represent.

Adaptation

Future adaptation can change the economic effect of a climate shock, so its treatment changes the estimated loss.

Discounting

Losses occurring later receive a different value today, according to the chosen discounting rule.

THE KEY DISTINCTION FOR LONG-RUN GROWTH

Output level

Output shifts downward. Once the climate stabilises, it can grow again at its earlier rate from this lower level.

Growth rate

The rate of output growth changes, so the loss continues through the future path.

Next step

Technology is no longer simply given: the next model makes the direction of innovation a choice.

Climate policy with directed technical change

Directed innovation

Innovation responds to economic incentives

In DICE, technological change affects production and abatement costs but is exogenous: the model does not determine its direction. The policy response is therefore a Pigovian tax on greenhouse-gas emissions that rises over time. Here, researchers choose which technology to improve, and their decision responds to the profit created by a successful innovation.

Climate policy can therefore affect emissions in two ways. It changes production incentives today, and it changes which technologies researchers find worthwhile to improve for the future.

AABH

How policy redirects innovation

Objective

2

3

Improve one variety

KEY EQUATION · QUALITY OF ONE VARIETY
$$A_{jit}=\begin{cases}(1+\gamma)A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if successful},\\A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if unsuccessful}.\end{cases}$$
KEY EQUATION · AVERAGE SECTOR QUALITY
$$A_{jt}=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Split varieties into two groups
$$\text{unchanged share}=1-h_js_{jt},\qquad\text{improved share}=h_js_{jt}. $$
2 · Add their average qualities
$$A_{jt}=(1-h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}+h_js_{jt}(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}. $$
3 · Factor out inherited average quality
$$\begin{aligned}A_{jt}&=\left[1-h_js_{jt}+h_js_{jt}+\gamma h_js_{jt}\right]A_{j,t-1}\\&=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

$h_js_{jt}$ is the fraction of varieties that both receive research and improve. The time index appears because today’s average quality is built from yesterday’s one.

Machines

Machine demand, monopoly quantity and profit

Objective

Find the demand faced by one machine monopolist, then solve that monopolist’s choice of $x_{ji}$.

KEY EQUATION · MACHINE DEMAND
$$p_{ji}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$
1 · State the competitive input-sector condition
$$p_{ji}=p_j\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}.$$
2 · Differentiate one variety, holding all other inputs fixed
$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}L_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta(1-\beta)x_{ji}^{-\beta}. $$
3 · Cancel $1-\beta$
$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=L_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}.$$
$$\boxed{p_{ji}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}.}$$
KEY EQUATION · MONOPOLIST’S QUANTITY
$$x_{ji}=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$
1 · State the monopolist’s objective
$$\max_{x_{ji}}\ \pi_{ji}=p_{ji}(x_{ji})x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}. $$
$$\pi_{ji}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}. $$
2 · Differentiate with respect to $x_{ji}$
$$\frac{\partial\pi_{ji}}{\partial x_{ji}}=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}-(1-\beta)=0. $$
3 · Divide by $1-\beta$ and isolate the power of $x_{ji}$
$$p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}=1$$
$$x_{ji}^\beta=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta. $$
4 · Raise both sides to $1/\beta$
$$\begin{aligned}x_{ji}&=\left(p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta\right)^{1/\beta}\\&=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}.\end{aligned}$$
KEY EQUATION · MONOPOLY PROFIT
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$
1 · Use the quantity first-order condition
$$p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}=1.$$
$$p_{ji}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}=1. $$
2 · Substitute this price into profit
$$\pi_{ji}=p_{ji}x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}=x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}=\beta x_{ji}. $$
3 · Insert the optimal quantity
$$\boxed{\pi_{ji}=\beta p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}.}$$
Economic interpretation

$p_j$ is the price of the intermediate input produced by sector $j$. $p_{ji}$ is the price charged for machine variety $i$. Better machines, a larger sector, or a higher input price raise the monopolist’s profit.

Sector output

Input production at the machine quantities chosen

Objective

Insert the optimal quantity of every machine into the sector production function and aggregate across varieties.

KEY EQUATION · SECTOR OUTPUT
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j$$
1 · Start from production and substitute the optimal machine quantity
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}L_j^\beta\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta\left(p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}\right)^{1-\beta}\,di. $$
2 · Apply the power $1-\beta$
$$\left(p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}\right)^{1-\beta}=p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j^{1-\beta}A_{ji}^{1-\beta}. $$
3 · Add exponents with the same base
$$L_j^\beta L_j^{1-\beta}=L_j^{\beta+1-\beta}=L_j,$$
$$A_{ji}^\beta A_{ji}^{1-\beta}=A_{ji}^{\beta+1-\beta}=A_{ji}. $$
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di. $$
4 · Define average machine quality
$$A_j\equiv\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di. $$
$$\boxed{Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j.}$$
Economic interpretation

The index $i$ disappears because the integral adds every machine variety. The sector index $j$ remains: clean and dirty sectors can have different prices, labour allocations and average quality.

Research reward

Expected profit from choosing a research sector

Objective

Find the expected private reward from sending a scientist to sector $j$.

KEY EQUATION · EXPECTED RESEARCH PROFIT
$$\Pi_{jt}=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Start from monopoly profit for one variety
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{ji}. $$
2 · Replace quality after a successful innovation
$$A_{ji}=(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}. $$
$$\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}=(1+\gamma)\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}. $$
3 · Weight the reward by the success probability
$$\Pi_{jt}=h_j\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}. $$
KEY EQUATION · SAME REWARD WRITTEN WITH SECTOR OUTPUT
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)(1-\beta)\beta p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}.$$
1 · Use the average-quality law
$$A_{j,t-1}=\frac{A_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}. $$
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}. $$
2 · Rewrite the price power
$$\frac{1}{\beta}=1+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}. $$
$$p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}=p_{jt}\left[p_{jt}^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_{jt}A_{jt}\right]. $$
3 · Use the sector-output equation
$$p_{jt}^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_{jt}A_{jt}=(1-\beta)Y_{jt}. $$
$$\boxed{\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)(1-\beta)\beta p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}.}$$
Economic interpretation

Research is more attractive when success is more likely, the machine improvement is larger, or the sector has a larger and more valuable market. The factor $(1+\gamma)(1-\beta)$ is common to both sectors and cancels in their profit ratio.

Objective

KEY EQUATION · RESEARCH-PROFIT RATIO

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c(1+\gamma)\beta p_{ct}^{1/\beta}L_{ct}A_{c,t-1}}{h_d(1+\gamma)\beta p_{dt}^{1/\beta}L_{dt}A_{d,t-1}}. $$
2 · Cancel common factors
$$\frac{(1+\gamma)\beta}{(1+\gamma)\beta}=1. $$
$$\frac{p_{ct}^{1/\beta}}{p_{dt}^{1/\beta}}=\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}. $$
3 · Keep the sector-specific terms
$$\boxed{\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}.}$$
$h_c/h_d$

Relative probability of success.

Price effect

Higher input price raises the reward from its machines.

Market-size effect

More labour means a larger market for machines.

Direct productivity effect

Better inherited machines are more profitable to improve.

Economic interpretation

If the ratio exceeds one, research goes clean; if it is below one, research goes dirty. Prices and labour are endogenous, so the next two results determine the price and market-size effects.

Equilibrium

Price and market size pull in different directions

Objective · price effect

Use labour mobility to derive the relative price of clean and dirty intermediate inputs.

KEY EQUATION · RELATIVE INPUT PRICE
$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}$$
1 · Start from the competitive wage
$$w_j=p_j\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial L_j}=\beta p_j\frac{Y_j}{L_j}. $$
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j. $$
2 · Substitute sector output into the wage
$$\begin{aligned}w_j&=\beta p_j\frac{\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j}{L_j}\\&=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_jp_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}A_j.\end{aligned}$$
3 · Add price exponents
$$1+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta}. $$
$$w_j=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_j^{1/\beta}A_j. $$
4 · Equalise wages across sectors
$$\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_c^{1/\beta}A_c=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_d^{1/\beta}A_d. $$
5 · Cancel the common factor and divide
$$p_c^{1/\beta}A_c=p_d^{1/\beta}A_d$$
$$\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{1/\beta}=\frac{A_d}{A_c}. $$
6 · Raise both sides to $\beta$
$$\boxed{\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_d}{A_c}\right)^\beta=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}.}$$
Economic interpretation · price effect

The more technologically advanced sector has a lower relative price. This pushes research away from the technological leader through the price term in expected profit.

Objective · market-size effect

Derive the final-good firm’s relative demand for clean and dirty intermediate inputs.

KEY EQUATION · CES RELATIVE DEMAND
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$
1 · State the firm’s choice

The final-good firm chooses the clean input $Y_c$ and the dirty input $Y_d$. The price of one unit of final output is normalised to one, so its profit is output minus the cost of the two inputs:

$$\pi^F=Y-p_cY_c-p_dY_d.$$

Its objective here is to obtain the relative demand:

$$\boxed{\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}.}$$
2 · Write the first-order conditions

At the optimum, the firm compares the extra final output created by one more unit of an input with the price paid for that input. For clean input:

$$\frac{\partial\pi^F}{\partial Y_c}=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p_c=\frac{\partial Y}{\partial Y_c}. $$

The same reasoning for dirty input gives:

$$p_d=\frac{\partial Y}{\partial Y_d}. $$
3 · Differentiate the CES production function

Start from:

$$Y=\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}.$$

To find $\partial Y/\partial Y_c$, hold $Y_d$ fixed and use the chain rule: differentiate the outside power, then the expression inside the brackets.

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Y}{\partial Y_c}={}&\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon-1}}\\&\times\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}$$

The two numerical coefficients cancel:

$$\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}\times\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}=1.$$
4 · Rewrite the remaining bracket

From the original production function:

$$Y^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}=Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}.$$

Therefore the bracket in the derivative can be written as $Y^{(\varepsilon-1)/\varepsilon}$. Raising it to $1/(\varepsilon-1)$ gives:

$$\left(Y^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon-1}}=Y^{1/\varepsilon}. $$

The clean first-order condition is thus:

$$\boxed{p_c=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}.}$$

Exactly the same calculation, now holding $Y_c$ fixed, gives:

$$\boxed{p_d=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}.}$$
5 · Divide the two first-order conditions

Put the clean condition over the dirty condition:

$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\frac{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}}{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}}.$$

The common factor $Y^{1/\varepsilon}$ cancels. Dividing the two remaining powers gives:

$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}\right)^{-1/\varepsilon}. $$
6 · Isolate the relative quantity

Raise both sides to the power $-\varepsilon$. On the right-hand side, the exponents multiply:

$$\left(\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}\right)^{(-1/\varepsilon)(-\varepsilon)}=\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}. $$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}.}$$

When the relative price of clean input falls, the final-good firm demands relatively more clean input.

Economic interpretation · CES demand

When clean input becomes relatively cheaper, the final-good firm demands relatively more clean input.

Objective · relative labour allocation

Combine sector output, CES demand and the relative price to obtain the market-size effect.

DEFINITION
$$\sigma\equiv1+\beta(\varepsilon-1),\qquad\sigma>1.$$
KEY EQUATION · RELATIVE LABOUR ALLOCATION
$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
1 · Divide the two sector-output equations
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}\frac{L_c}{L_d}\frac{A_c}{A_d}. $$
2 · Substitute the relative price
$$\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)}. $$
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)+1}\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}. $$
3 · Write CES demand with the same technology ratio
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{-\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}. $$
4 · Equate the two expressions and divide by the common power
$$\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}. $$
$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon-\beta}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta(\varepsilon-1)}. $$
5 · Use the definition of $\sigma$
$$\sigma-1=\beta(\varepsilon-1). $$
$$\boxed{\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1}.}$$
Economic interpretation · market-size effect

Because $\sigma-1>0$, the more advanced sector attracts more labour and therefore offers a larger market for machine innovation.

KEY EQUATION · PATH-DEPENDENT RESEARCH REWARD

1 · Insert the price and labour ratios
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1},\qquad\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-1}. $$

COMPARE THE TWO EXPECTED REWARDS
$$\frac{\Pi_{Pt}}{\Pi_{Et}}=\frac{h_P}{h_E}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_Es_{Et}}{1+\gamma h_Ps_{Pt}}\right)\left(\frac{LA_{Pt}}{EA_{Et}}\right)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}$$

Exponent

COMPLEMENTARITY MAKES THE EXPONENT NEGATIVE
$$\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1),\qquad 0<\sigma<1$$
1 · Apply the same definition of σ to this case
$$\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)=1-\beta(1-\varepsilon)$$

Here $0<\varepsilon<1$ and $0<\beta<1$. Therefore $0<\beta(1-\varepsilon)<1$, which gives $0<\sigma<1$.

The numerator is negative and the denominator positive. This explains the sign used when reading the research-profit ratio.

With $0<\sigma<1$, $(\sigma-1)/\sigma$ is negative. The relatively lagging input becomes more attractive for innovation.
