Growth, Natural Resources and Climate Change

Land and natural resources in a Solow model

Starting point

The Limits to Growth: the Meadows report

The Meadows report asks whether continuing economic and population growth is compatible with a finite supply of resources. Exponential growth means repeated proportional increases, while the resource stock available to support production is limited.

Its warning is conditional: without major changes in resource use and environmental destruction, population and industrial capacity may decline abruptly. The issue is therefore whether the way the economy uses resources can change enough to sustain growth.

DHSS

Can consumption be maintained despite depletion?

The Dasgupta–Heal–Solow–Stiglitz (DHSS) model responds to this concern. It studies the technological conditions under which consumption per person can be maintained even though the use of non-renewable resources must eventually decline.

Three possible ways to compensate

Input substitution allows production to replace some of the scarce resource with other inputs. How far firms can change this combination depends on the production technology.

Resource-augmenting technical progress makes each unit of the resource more productive. A smaller physical quantity can then provide more productive services than before.

Increasing returns to scale mean that production can rise more than proportionally when the scale of activity increases. This is the third mechanism identified in the course.

Production

Adding land and an exhaustible resource

Nordhaus adds fixed land and an exhaustible resource to the Solow production function:

$$Y=K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}$$

Goal: rewrite the same production function so that products become sums. All inputs are positive where we take logarithms; the exponents are constants.

1 · Take the natural logarithm of both sides
$$\ln Y=\ln\!\left[K^\alpha T^\beta R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right]$$

$\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm. At this stage, we only apply the same operation to both sides.

2 · Turn the product into a sum

Use $\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v$ on the four multiplied factors:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\ln(K^\alpha)+\ln(T^\beta)\\&+\ln(R^\gamma)+\ln\!\left[(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\right].\end{aligned}$$
3 · Bring each power in front of its logarithm

Use $\ln(u^a)=a\ln u$. For example, $\ln(K^\alpha)=\alpha\ln K$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\ln(AL).\end{aligned}$$
4 · Split effective labour into A and L
$$\ln(AL)=\ln A+\ln L$$
$$\begin{aligned}\ln Y={}&\alpha\ln K+\beta\ln T+\gamma\ln R\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(\ln A+\ln L).\end{aligned}$$

Every term comes from one factor of the original function. We have rewritten output; we have not differentiated yet.

$Y$, $K$, $L$

Output, capital and labour.

$A$ and $AL$

Labour-augmenting productivity and effective labour.

$T$

Land: its quantity is fixed.

$S$ and $R$

The remaining resource stock and the extraction flow used in production.

The resource stock and the resource flow

$S$ is what remains available. $R$ is what is used per unit of time. Production depends on the flow $R$, while extraction changes the stock $S$. Keeping these two quantities distinct is essential for reading the model.

The production exponents

The exponents $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ determine how capital, land and resource use enter the Cobb–Douglas production function. The remaining exponent, $1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$, applies to effective labour.

$$\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}=g_A,\qquad \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}=n,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}=0$$

Productivity

$A$ grows at the given rate $g_A$.

Population

$L$ grows at rate $n$.

Land

$T$ does not grow.

Resource use

A constant proportional extraction rate

$$\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS,\qquad \frac{1}{S}\frac{dS}{dt}=-q$$

Assumption

The extraction rate $q$ is constant: each period uses the same fraction of the remaining stock.

Mechanism

A smaller stock means a smaller extraction flow, even with the same proportional rate.

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Start from the extraction rule $R=qS$, with $q$ constant.

1 · Differentiate the extraction rule
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=q\frac{dS}{dt}=q(-qS)$$
2 · Use R = qS
$$\frac{dR}{dt}=-qR\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}=-q$$

Both the stock and the extraction flow decline at the same proportional rate.

Fixing $q$ simplifies the model. Choosing resource use optimally across dates is the problem introduced by Hotelling.

Growth

The economy’s regular growth rate

Each input contributes its growth rate, weighted by its production exponent. Land does not grow; resource use grows at $-q$.

$$g_Y=\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
1 · Why differentiate a logarithm?

Each variable depends on time. The chain rule gives the derivative of $\ln X(t)$:

$$\frac{d\ln X(t)}{dt}=\frac{1}{X(t)}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}\equiv g_X.$$

$dX/dt$ is the change in the level. Dividing by $X$ makes it a proportional growth rate.

2 · Differentiate the logged production function

The exponents are constant, so they remain in front of the derivatives. Apply the same chain rule to every logarithm:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt}={}&\alpha\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}+\beta\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}\\&+\gamma\frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dt}\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)\left(\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}+\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}\right).\end{aligned}$$
3 · Replace each proportional derivative by its name
$$g_Y=\alpha g_K+\beta g_T+\gamma g_R+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+g_L)$$

This is why each input’s growth rate is multiplied by its production exponent.

4 · Substitute the model’s assumptions
$$g_T=0,\qquad g_R=-q,\qquad g_L=n$$
$$\begin{aligned}g_Y={}&\alpha g_K+\beta(0)+\gamma(-q)\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&\alpha g_K-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n).\end{aligned}$$

Fixed land removes the $\beta g_T$ term. Declining resource use produces the negative term $-\gamma q$.

With a constant saving rate $s$ and capital depreciation $\delta$:

$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
1 · Divide every term of accumulation by K
$$\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=\frac{sY}{K}-\frac{\delta K}{K}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$

The left side is $g_K$. Capital cancels in the depreciation term.

2 · Isolate the output–capital ratio
$$\frac{Y}{K}=\frac{g_K+\delta}{s}$$

On a regular growth path, $g_K$ is constant. With constant $s>0$ and $\delta$, the right side is constant, so $Y/K$ is constant.

3 · Differentiate the logarithm of that constant ratio
$$\ln(Y/K)=\ln Y-\ln K$$
$$0=\frac{d\ln(Y/K)}{dt}=g_Y-g_K$$
$$g_Y=g_K$$

The zero comes from differentiating a constant. Output and capital must therefore grow at the same proportional rate.

1

Regular growth

Capital grows at a constant rate.

2

Constant output–capital ratio

$Y/K$ stays constant.

3

Same growth rate

Output and capital grow together: $g_Y=g_K$.

$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Substitute gK = gY
$$g_Y=\alpha g_Y-\gamma q+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)$$
2 · Subtract αgY from both sides
$$g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$
$$(1-\alpha)g_Y=(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q$$

Factor out $g_Y$ on the left: $g_Y-\alpha g_Y=(1-\alpha)g_Y$.

3 · Divide both sides by 1 − α
$$g_Y=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
4 · Read the condition for positive growth

Since $0<\alpha<1$, the denominator is positive. The fraction is positive exactly when its numerator is positive:

$$\begin{aligned}g_Y>0\quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q>0\\&\Longleftrightarrow\quad(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q.\end{aligned}$$
Positive growth

The contribution of effective labour must exceed the loss from resource depletion.

$$(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)>\gamma q$$

If depletion is too fast, the condition fails.

Living standards

Growth of GDP per person

From total GDP to GDP per person

Subtract population growth: $g_Y-n$.

$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · GDP per person is Y divided by L
$$\ln(Y/L)=\ln Y-\ln L$$
$$\frac{d\ln(Y/L)}{dt}=g_Y-g_L=g_Y-n$$

The subtraction comes from the quotient $Y/L$. It is the growth rate of that quotient, not a subtraction from the level of output.

2 · Give both terms the same denominator
$$n=\frac{n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)-\gamma q-n(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$
3 · Expand and collect only the terms in n
$$\begin{aligned}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)n-n(1-\alpha)\\&\quad=n-\alpha n-\beta n-\gamma n-n+\alpha n\\&\quad=-\beta n-\gamma n=-(\beta+\gamma)n.\end{aligned}$$

The $n$ terms cancel, and so do the $\alpha n$ terms. The terms involving $g_A$ and $q$ are unchanged.

4 · Put the remaining terms together
$$g_Y-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$

This is the per-capita growth formula shown above.

Condition to retain

Productivity must grow fast enough to offset population growth and the declining resource flow.

Growth drag

The drag on economic growth

The growth drag is the reduction in per-capita growth caused by the resource constraints.

Comparison path

Land and resource use grow with population.

Constrained path

Land is fixed and resource use declines.

$$\mathrm{drag}=\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}$$
1 · Write growth on the comparison path

In this comparison, land and resource use both grow with population: $g_T=g_R=n$. Use the same production function and regular-growth condition $g_K=g_Y$:

$$\begin{aligned}(1-\alpha)g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}={}&\beta n+\gamma n\\&+(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)(g_A+n)\\={}&(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A+(1-\alpha)n.\end{aligned}$$
2 · Convert the comparison to per-capita growth
$$g_Y^{\mathrm{comparison}}-n=\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}$$

Divide the previous line by $1-\alpha$, then subtract $n$. Its population term cancels.

3 · Subtract constrained growth from comparison growth
$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{drag}={}&\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A}{1-\alpha}\\&-\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta-\gamma)g_A-(\beta+\gamma)n-\gamma q}{1-\alpha}\\={}&\frac{(\beta+\gamma)n+\gamma q}{1-\alpha}.\end{aligned}$$

The productivity terms cancel. The minus sign before the second fraction turns its two negative terms into positive terms.

Reading the sign

The drag is a positive gap: it measures how much the constraints reduce growth.

Substitution

Why the elasticity of substitution matters

The Cobb–Douglas production function has an elasticity of substitution equal to one. It allows firms to compensate for a scarcer resource by changing their combination of inputs. If this elasticity is below one, replacing the resource becomes more difficult.

As the resource becomes scarcer, a growing share of income goes to it. That share tends towards one, while the shares received by the other inputs become small. The constraint imposed by the resource therefore becomes increasingly important for growth.

In the limiting outcome described in the course, the growth drag approaches $n+q$, and GDP per person falls at the rate $n+q$.

Hotelling

Choosing resource use across time

Starting point

The resource’s income share is constant, while the quantity used falls. The resource price rises.

What is missing?

Fixing extraction $q$ and the saving rate does not establish an optimal allocation.

Hotelling’s problem: choose consumption and resource use together, accounting for capital accumulation and depletion.

$$\max\int_0^\infty\frac{C(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=Y-C-\delta K,\qquad \frac{dS}{dt}=-R$$
$$Y=K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}$$

Consumption and discounting

$C$ is consumption. Its utility is added across dates, with $e^{-\rho t}$ giving less weight to later utility. The parameter $\rho$ is the rate of time preference; $\varepsilon$ determines the curvature of consumption utility.

Capital accumulation

Output left after consumption and depreciation adds to capital. The first constraint records that allocation.

Resource depletion

Using $R$ units of the resource reduces the remaining stock by the same amount. The production function in this problem includes the resource flow and no separate land term.

Hotelling exercise

Two controls, two states, four conditions

CONTROL VARIABLES · choices

$C$ = consumption.
$R$ = resource use.
Choose their values at each date.

STATE VARIABLES · stocks

$K$ = capital.
$S$ = resource left.
They change through accumulation and extraction.

The current-value Hamiltonian adds current utility and the value of changes in both stocks:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal H={}&\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\\&+\lambda_K\left[K^\alpha R^\gamma(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}-C-\delta K\right]\\&-\lambda_SR.\end{aligned}$$

Capital

$\lambda_K$: the implicit price of one more unit of capital.

Resource

$\lambda_S$: the implicit price of one more unit of the resource left in the ground.

An implicit price measures how much an extra unit of a stock is worth in this optimisation problem.

A · FOCs for CONTROL VARIABLES: C and R

For each control, set its partial derivative to zero: $\partial\mathcal H/\partial C=0$ and $\partial\mathcal H/\partial R=0$.

1 · Consumption C
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial C}=C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0$$
Meaning

The gain from consuming one more unit equals the value of keeping that unit as capital: $C^{-\varepsilon}=\lambda_K$.

Differentiate the two terms containing C
$$\frac{d}{dC}\frac{C^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{\partial(-\lambda_K C)}{\partial C}=-\lambda_K$$
Set the derivative to zero
$$C^{-\varepsilon}-\lambda_K=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_K=C^{-\varepsilon}$$
2 · Resource use R
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S=0$$
Meaning

Using one more unit of the resource creates output worth $\lambda_K\gamma Y/R$, but removes a unit from the stock worth $\lambda_S$. At the optimum, gain = cost.

Differentiate production
$$\frac{\partial Y}{\partial R}=\gamma K^\alpha R^{\gamma-1}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\gamma}=\gamma\frac{Y}{R}$$
Include the loss from extraction
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial R}=\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}-\lambda_S$$
$$\lambda_K\gamma\frac{Y}{R}=\lambda_S$$

B · Conditions for STATE VARIABLES: K and S

Use the costate equations for their implicit prices. Do not set the stock derivatives of $\mathcal H$ to zero.

3 · Capital stock K
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K$$
Meaning

The value of capital changes with impatience $\rho$, depreciation $\delta$ and its marginal product $\alpha Y/K$.

Differentiate the Hamiltonian with respect to K
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}=\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)$$
Use the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}=\rho\lambda_K-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial K}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d\lambda_K}{dt}&=\rho\lambda_K-\lambda_K\left(\alpha\frac{Y}{K}-\delta\right)\\&=\left(\rho+\delta-\alpha\frac{Y}{K}\right)\lambda_K.\end{aligned}$$
4 · Resource stock S
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Meaning

The Hamiltonian contains the extraction flow $R$, but no direct term in the remaining stock $S$. Therefore $\partial\mathcal H/\partial S=0$.

Apply the current-value costate rule
$$\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}$$
Use the absence of a direct S term
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho\lambda_S$$
Divide by the implicit price of the resource to obtain its growth rate
$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$
Result to remember

The resource’s current-value implicit price grows at the rate of time preference $\rho$.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda_S}\frac{d\lambda_S}{dt}=\rho$$

Economic meaning

Keeping a unit in the ground allows it to be used later. Its future implicit price must compensate for the delay in utility.

Why the stock still matters

Extraction removes future resources. This cost is recorded by $-\lambda_SR$, even though $S$ does not appear directly in the Hamiltonian.

Which price?

This result concerns $\lambda_S$, the implicit price of the resource in this exercise.

Historical discussion

Wood, coal and the timing of resource use

The transition from wood to coal can be understood in two ways. They differ in which event causes the other.

Scarcity encourages innovation

In the first interpretation, deforestation exhausts wood and creates a need for another energy source. Coal combustion then provides an alternative. Depletion comes before the search for a replacement.

An alternative changes the incentive to conserve

In the interpretation associated with Dasgupta and Stiglitz, coal use is already known but initially costs more than wood. Once it becomes an available alternative, energy supply depends less on scarce wood.

The incentive to preserve forests weakens, so deforestation can occur. Here, access to the alternative helps explain depletion.

Discussion: electricity storage and oil

If the second interpretation applies to electricity storage and oil, what could happen once storage becomes available? Is this outcome likely, and what could prevent the problem? These remain open questions.

Natural resources in endogenous growth models

AK model

Why the resource constraint ends permanent AK growth

Add the resource flow $R$ to AK production, with $0<\gamma<1$ and a constant saving rate $s$:

$$Y=AKR^\gamma$$
$$\frac{dK}{dt}=sY-\delta K,\qquad\frac{dS}{dt}=-R=-qS$$
$$g_K=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
1 · Divide accumulation by capital
$$g_K=\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt}=s\frac{Y}{K}-\delta$$
2 · Insert resource-dependent production
$$g_K=s\frac{AKR^\gamma}{K}-\delta=sAR^\gamma-\delta$$
3 · Read the effect of declining resource use

As $R$ falls, $R^\gamma$ becomes smaller. The positive accumulation term shrinks, while depreciation remains. Eventually $g_K$ becomes negative and tends towards $-\delta$.

Result

The declining resource term eventually outweighs accumulation. Permanent AK growth disappears; capital and production contract.

The DICE model and climate policy

DICE

Linking the world economy and the climate

The Dynamic Integrated Climate–Economy model (DICE) starts from the economy and adds the climate channel missing from a standard growth model. Production creates emissions; emissions affect the climate; and climate damage reduces the output available to the economy.

STARTProductioncreates emissions
THENEmissionschange the climate
FINALLYClimate damagereduces available output
The policy question

Abatement costs output now, but lower emissions avoid future damage. The planner chooses the path that weighs these two effects.

Objective

Discounted economic welfare

The planner compares welfare at different dates. Consumption gives utility per person, population turns this into total welfare, discounting gives future welfare a different weight, and the integral adds these weighted values through time.

THE PLANNER ADDS WELFARE ACROSS TIME
$$W=\int_{2020}\frac{c(t)^{1-\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon}L(t)e^{-(\rho+pr+ad)}$$
Consumption utility

$c(t)^{1-\epsilon}/(1-\epsilon)$: welfare per person.

Population

$L(t)$: turns per-person welfare into total welfare.

Discounting

$e^{-(\rho+pr+ad)}$: gives a weight to future welfare.

Integral

Adds weighted welfare over time.

The term $\rho$ captures preference for utility sooner rather than later. The terms $pr$ and $ad$ adjust this discounting for the risks represented in the model. The next equation determines the output that can be consumed once damage and abatement costs are taken into account.

Model differences

Why do damage estimates differ so much?

An SCC is not a single mechanical number. It changes with the climate effects included, the way warming becomes damage, the role of adaptation and the weight attached to future losses.

Innovation

Scientists improve machine quality

Research object

Research improves machine quality. Scientists choose a sector; $s_{jt}$ is the share choosing sector $j$, then random assignment selects a machine variety.

1

Choose a sector

Scientists choose clean or dirty research. Their total mass is one.

2

Attempt an improvement

In sector $j$, a research attempt succeeds with probability $h_j$.

3

Change machine quality

On success, the assigned machine’s quality is multiplied by $1+\gamma$.

Success probability

$h_j$ gives the chance that a research attempt succeeds.

Quality improvement

A success multiplies quality by $1+\gamma$, with $\gamma>0$.

Private reward

A successful innovator receives monopoly rights for one period.

For one variety, the quality rule is therefore:

$$A_{jit}=\begin{cases}(1+\gamma)A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if successful},\\A_{ji,t-1}&\text{if unsuccessful}.\end{cases}$$

The economy uses the average quality of varieties in a sector. Because only the fraction $h_js_{jt}$ both chooses sector $j$ and succeeds, average quality evolves as:

$$A_{jt}=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Identify the fraction of machines improved

Research effort is $s_{jt}$ and its success probability is $h_j$. With random assignment, the fraction improved is $h_js_{jt}$; the fraction unchanged is $1-h_js_{jt}$.

2 · Add unchanged and improved quality
$$\begin{aligned}A_{jt}={}&(1-h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}\\&+h_js_{jt}(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

Random assignment lets both groups start from the same inherited average quality. Successful research multiplies that quality by $1+\gamma$.

3 · Factor out inherited quality and simplify
$$\begin{aligned}A_{jt}&=\left[1-h_js_{jt}+h_js_{jt}+\gamma h_js_{jt}\right]A_{j,t-1}\\&=(1+\gamma h_js_{jt})A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The terms $-h_js_{jt}$ and $+h_js_{jt}$ cancel. The remaining addition is the quality gain.

Assumptions

A technological lead for the dirty sector

The quality law tells us how research changes a sector’s future technology. To determine its direction, we also need the starting position. The model assumes no cross-sector spillovers: clean research improves clean machines, and dirty research improves dirty machines.

It also begins with a sufficiently large dirty-technology advantage:

$$A_{d0}\text{ is sufficiently higher than }A_{c0}$$

This is the catch-up problem. The dirty sector starts with more productive machines, and we must now determine whether private incentives lead researchers to strengthen that advantage or to improve clean technology instead.

Machines

Machine demand, monopoly quantity and profit

Input producerchooses how much of machine $i$ to use
→
Competitive ruleprice = value of one more machine
→
Machine demandtells the monopolist what buyers will pay
$$p_{ji}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}$$
1 · Differentiate the contribution of machine i

Hold labour, technology and the other machine quantities fixed. In the production integral, the quantity of this machine appears as $x_{ji}^{1-\beta}$:

$$\frac{d(x_{ji}^{1-\beta})}{dx_{ji}}=(1-\beta)x_{ji}^{-\beta}.$$
2 · Cancel the two opposite factors

The production function contains the prefactor $1/(1-\beta)$. It cancels the factor from the power rule:

$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}L_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta(1-\beta)x_{ji}^{-\beta}=L_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}.$$
$$p_{ji}=p_j\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial x_{ji}}=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}.$$

$p_j$ converts the extra sector output into revenue.

Monopolist’s choice

Choose $x_{ji}$ to maximise revenue minus cost.

Unit cost

Each machine costs $1-\beta$ final goods.

$$MC=1-\beta$$

Optimal quantity

How much of variety $i$ the monopolist supplies.

$$x_{ji}=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$

Monopoly profit

The prize from owning that variety.

$$\pi_{ji}=\beta p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$$
1 · Write profit as a function of machine quantity
$$\begin{aligned}\pi_{ji}(x_{ji})&=p_{ji}(x_{ji})x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}\\&=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}.\end{aligned}$$

Revenue is price times quantity. Cost is the unit cost $1-\beta$ times quantity.

2 · Differentiate and set the derivative to zero
$$\frac{d\pi_{ji}}{dx_{ji}}=(1-\beta)p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}-(1-\beta)=0.$$

Divide by $1-\beta>0$:

$$p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{-\beta}=1.$$
3 · Isolate the machine quantity
$$x_{ji}^\beta=p_jL_j^\beta A_{ji}^\beta$$
$$x_{ji}=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}.$$

Multiply by $x_{ji}^\beta$, then raise both sides to $1/\beta$.

4 · Insert the optimal quantity into profit

The left side of the first-order condition is exactly $p_{ji}$. At the chosen quantity, $p_{ji}=1$, so:

$$\pi_{ji}=x_{ji}-(1-\beta)x_{ji}=\beta x_{ji}$$
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}. $$

Two different prices

$p_j$ is the price of the clean or dirty intermediate input. $p_{ji}$ is the price of one particular machine variety. The first appears in the buyer’s revenue; the second is what the machine producer charges.

Why the chosen quantity has this form

The formula for $x_{ji}$ contains exactly the objects that make this variety valuable to its buyer: the sector price, sector labour and the machine’s quality. A larger value of any of these raises the monopoly quantity.

Why profit matters

$\pi_{ji}$ is the machine producer’s monopoly profit. In the next step, this is the prize a scientist can obtain by successfully improving a machine variety.

The $1/(1-\beta)$ prefactor affects the machine-demand derivative, while the unit cost remains $1-\beta$. This combination gives $x_{ji}=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$.

Sector output

Input production at the machine quantities chosen

We have solved the machine producer’s problem variety by variety. We now return to the sector production function and ask what its output is when every machine is used at that chosen quantity. This is a substitution into the original technology, not a new production function:

$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j$$
1 · Start from the sector production function
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}L_j^\beta\int_0^1 A_{ji}^\beta x_{ji}^{1-\beta}\,di.$$

Insert $x_{ji}=p_j^{1/\beta}L_jA_{ji}$.

2 · Apply the power 1 − β to every factor
$$x_{ji}^{1-\beta}=p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j^{1-\beta}A_{ji}^{1-\beta}.$$
3 · Collect equal bases and take common factors outside
$$L_j^\beta L_j^{1-\beta}=L_j,\qquad A_{ji}^\beta A_{ji}^{1-\beta}=A_{ji}.$$
$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_j\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di.$$

The price and labour input do not depend on the machine index $i$, so they come outside the integral.

4 · Use average machine quality
$$A_j\equiv\int_0^1 A_{ji}\,di.$$

The machine interval has total length one, so this integral is average quality. Therefore:

$$Y_j=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}L_jA_j.$$
From one variety to one sector

$A_j$ is average machine quality. Once all varieties are collected, sector output depends on its price, labour and average technology.

The index $i$ disappears because we have added across all machine varieties. The index $j$ remains because clean and dirty production still have different prices, labour allocations and technology levels. This sector-level output will now be used to express the reward from research.

The factor $1/(1-\beta)$ remains because it belongs to the original production function.

Research reward

Expected profit from choosing a research sector

The reason for calculating machine monopoly profit now becomes clear: a successful scientist obtains the monopoly right over the improved machine. A scientist therefore compares the expected value of that prize in the clean and dirty sectors.

If research succeeds

The improved variety has quality $(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}$.

Expected reward

Multiply the monopoly profit from that improved variety by the probability of success.

Using the monopoly-profit formula already found gives:

$$\Pi_{jt}=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}$$
1 · Start from the machine producer’s profit
$$\pi_{ji}=\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{ji}. $$
2 · Use quality after a successful innovation

Random assignment gives inherited average quality $A_{j,t-1}$. Conditional on success, multiply it by $1+\gamma$:

$$\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}=\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}. $$
3 · Weight this reward by success probability
$$\begin{aligned}\Pi_{jt}&=h_j\pi_{jt}^{\mathrm{success}}\\&=h_j(1+\gamma)\beta p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{j,t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The expected reward includes the monopoly right obtained if research succeeds.

Success

$h_j$ appears because an innovation is valuable only when it succeeds.

Improved technology

$(1+\gamma)A_{j,t-1}$ is the quality available after success. The researcher builds on the technology inherited from the previous date.

Market value

$p_{jt}$ and $L_{jt}$ describe the market in which the improved machine is sold. They matter because machine profit depends on how valuable the sector is to its buyers.

Sometimes it is more useful to write the same reward using sector output rather than average quality. Replacing inherited quality through the quality law and then using the sector-output expression gives:

$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)(1-\beta)\beta p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}.$$
1 · Express inherited quality in terms of current quality
$$A_{j,t-1}=\frac{A_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}$$
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}. $$
2 · Match the power appearing in sector output
$$\frac{1}{\beta}=1+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}.$$
$$p_{jt}^{1/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}=(1-\beta)p_{jt}Y_{jt}.$$

This follows from $Y_{jt}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}p_{jt}^{(1-\beta)/\beta}L_{jt}A_{jt}$.

3 · Substitute sector output
$$\Pi_{jt}=\frac{h_j(1+\gamma)\beta(1-\beta)p_{jt}Y_{jt}}{1+\gamma h_js_{jt}}.$$

This is the expression implied by the preceding production function and monopoly-profit equations.

Consistency check. The factor $(1+\gamma)(1-\beta)$ makes this output-based expression exactly equal to the first research-profit formula. It is common to clean and dirty research and therefore cancels from their profit ratio.

Research choice

Where will scientists choose to innovate?

A scientist does not need the absolute reward in each sector to choose between them. Comparing clean and dirty expected profits as a ratio is enough: a ratio above one favours clean research, while a ratio below one favours dirty research.

Dividing the two reward expressions cancels what is common to both sectors and leaves the forces that differ between them:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
1 · Write the numerator and denominator
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c(1+\gamma)\beta p_{ct}^{1/\beta}L_{ct}A_{c,t-1}}{h_d(1+\gamma)\beta p_{dt}^{1/\beta}L_{dt}A_{d,t-1}}.$$
2 · Cancel the factors common to both sectors

$1+\gamma$ and $\beta$ cancel. The price terms combine into a relative price:

$$\frac{p_{ct}^{1/\beta}}{p_{dt}^{1/\beta}}=\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}.$$
3 · Keep the four sector-specific ratios
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}.$$

The terms remaining are success probability, price, labour and inherited quality.

Ratio > 1

Research goes to the clean sector.

Ratio < 1

Research goes to the dirty sector.

Ratio = 1

Scientists may work in both sectors.

Success probabilities

$h_c/h_d$ compares the chance of a successful innovation in the two sectors.

Price effect

$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}$$

A machine is more profitable when the intermediate input it helps produce has a higher price. We therefore need equilibrium prices before interpreting this term.

Market-size effect

$$\frac{L_{ct}}{L_{dt}}$$

Labour measures the scale of each intermediate sector. A larger sector uses more machines, so it creates a larger market for an innovation.

Direct productivity effect

$$\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

Innovation improves the quality already in place. This directly favours the sector that begins with higher technology.

The ratio is not yet fully informative because prices and labour are themselves determined inside the economy. We find those equilibrium relationships next.

Equilibrium

Price and market size pull in different directions

Labour can work in either intermediate sector, so its allocation is endogenous. In equilibrium, a worker must create the same value in the clean and dirty sectors; otherwise labour would move towards the sector paying more. Equating those marginal values gives the relative-price relationship:

$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}$$
1 · Differentiate sector production with respect to labour

Use the original production function, holding the machine quantities fixed in this partial derivative:

$$\frac{\partial Y_j}{\partial L_j}=\beta\frac{Y_j}{L_j}.$$

The prefactor $1/(1-\beta)$ is already contained in $Y_j$. The value of this marginal product is the wage:

$$w_j=\beta p_j\frac{Y_j}{L_j}. $$
2 · Insert equilibrium sector output
$$\begin{aligned}w_j&=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_jp_j^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}A_j\\&=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}p_j^{1/\beta}A_j.\end{aligned}$$

The price powers add up: $1+(1-\beta)/\beta=1/\beta$.

3 · Set clean and dirty wages equal

Labour can move across sectors. Equating the two marginal revenues and cancelling their common coefficient gives:

$$p_c^{1/\beta}A_c=p_d^{1/\beta}A_d.$$
$$\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{1/\beta}=\frac{A_d}{A_c}. $$
4 · Raise both sides to β
$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\left(\frac{A_d}{A_c}\right)^\beta=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}. $$

The negative exponent appears because the technology ratio is inverted.

Price effect → less advanced sector

The more advanced sector has the lower price. Through the price term in research profits, this works against its technological advantage.

The negative exponent is the key. If clean technology is relatively high, its price is relatively low. This makes innovation in the advanced sector less attractive through the price channel.

Final-good producers also choose how much clean and dirty input to buy. Their competitive demand links relative quantities to the relative prices just found:

$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$
1 · Differentiate with respect to each input

Competitive final-good producers equate input prices with marginal products, taking the final-good price as one. For the clean input:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Y}{\partial Y_c}={}&\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}\left(Y_c^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}+Y_d^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon-1}}\\&\times\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}$$

The two fractions cancel. The bracket to power $1/(\varepsilon-1)$ equals $Y^{1/\varepsilon}$, so:

$$p_c=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon},\qquad p_d=Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}.$$
2 · Divide the two marginal products
$$\frac{p_c}{p_d}=\frac{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_c^{-1/\varepsilon}}{Y^{1/\varepsilon}Y_d^{-1/\varepsilon}}=\left(\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}\right)^{-1/\varepsilon}$$

The common factor involving total output cancels.

3 · Isolate the input-quantity ratio

Raise both sides to $-\varepsilon$. The exponents multiply to one: $(-1/\varepsilon)(-\varepsilon)=1$.

$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{-\varepsilon}$$

We can now combine final-good demand, sector output and relative prices. This replaces the unobserved output ratio with the technology ratio and gives the allocation of labour:

$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\sigma-1},\qquad\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)>1$$
1 · Divide the sector-output expressions
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{p_c}{p_d}\right)^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}\frac{L_c}{L_d}\frac{A_c}{A_d}. $$

The common production prefactor $1/(1-\beta)$ cancels from this ratio.

2 · Substitute the relative price and multiply exponents
$$\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)}.$$
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-(1-\beta)+1}\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}. $$

The extra $+1$ comes from the technology factor already present in the output ratio.

3 · Express final-good demand using the same technology ratio
$$\frac{Y_c}{Y_d}=\left[\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{-\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}. $$
4 · Equate the two expressions and isolate labour
$$\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^\beta\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon}.$$
$$\frac{L_c}{L_d}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta\varepsilon-\beta}=\left(\frac{A_c}{A_d}\right)^{\beta(\varepsilon-1)}.$$

Dividing powers with the same base subtracts their exponents. Since $\sigma=1+\beta(\varepsilon-1)$, the exponent is $\sigma-1$.

Market-size effect → more advanced sector

Because $\sigma-1>0$, the more advanced sector attracts more labour. This market-size force pushes innovation towards the technological leader.

Laissez-faire

Path dependence in the direction of innovation

We can now return to the research-profit ratio. The equilibrium relationships just found tell us how the price and market-size terms depend on technology. Substituting them into the research choice gives:

$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$
1 · Insert price and labour ratios into research profits
$$\left(\frac{p_{ct}}{p_{dt}}\right)^{1/\beta}=\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1}$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-1}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$

The two current-technology exponents add: $-1+(\sigma-1)=\sigma-2$. Keep the inherited ratio separate.

2 · Replace current quality using the accumulation equations
$$\frac{A_{ct}}{A_{dt}}=\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left[\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right]^{\sigma-2}\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}$$
3 · Apply the power to both factors inside the brackets
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^1$$
4 · Add the inherited-technology exponents
$$(\sigma-2)+1=\sigma-1$$
$$\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}=\frac{h_c}{h_d}\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}$$

The exponent on inherited technology is $\sigma-1>0$ in the substitutable-input case.

Which force wins?

The direct productivity and market-size effects dominate the price effect. Innovation therefore tends towards the sector already ahead.

Path dependence

A sufficiently large technological lead today makes further research in the same direction more profitable tomorrow. The initial advantage thus shapes later research and can reinforce itself.

With an initial dirty lead, private research incentives do not automatically redirect innovation towards clean production. We now add the environmental cost that private research leaves out.

Environment

Dirty production depletes environmental quality

Private research rewards only value the machine market. They do not include the environmental cost of dirty output. The model represents environmental quality as a stock that is depleted by dirty production but also regenerates:

$$S_{t+1}=-\chi Y_{dt}+(1+\delta)S_t$$

Dirty-production term

$-\chi Y_{dt}$ lowers next period’s environmental quality. The coefficient $\chi$ converts dirty output into environmental depletion.

Stock and regeneration

$(1+\delta)S_t$ carries the existing stock into the next period and includes regeneration at rate $\delta>0$. Here $\delta$ describes environmental regeneration, not capital depreciation.

Boundary

$\bar S$ is preindustrial environmental quality. At $S=0$, the model imposes an absorbing boundary: environmental quality no longer recovers.

Laissez-faire outcome

If dirty technologies begin far ahead, dirty output and CO₂ emissions grow without bound in the model. Environmental quality is then depleted.

Policy instruments

Redirecting innovation towards clean technologies

Policy must change the private rewards that drive research. The model uses two instruments: a clean-research subsidy, $q_t$, and a carbon tax, $\tau_t$.

$q_t$ · Research subsidy

Raises the reward from clean research.

$\tau_t$ · Carbon tax

Changes the price incentive attached to dirty production.

Here $q_t$ denotes the research subsidy. The two instruments enter the clean-to-dirty research-profit ratio as follows:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\Pi_{ct}}{\Pi_{dt}}={}&(1+q_t)(1+\tau_t)^\varepsilon\frac{h_c}{h_d}\\&\times\left(\frac{1+\gamma h_cs_{ct}}{1+\gamma h_ds_{dt}}\right)^{\sigma-2}\left(\frac{A_{c,t-1}}{A_{d,t-1}}\right)^{\sigma-1}.\end{aligned}$$

The factor $1+q_t$ acts directly on the reward to clean research. It is multiplied into the ratio because a subsidy raises the payoff from a successful clean innovation relative to dirty innovation.

The tax changes the price incentive attached to dirty production. For given technologies, it lowers the price received by the dirty producer. Its combined effect on the research comparison is represented by $(1+\tau_t)^\varepsilon$.

1

Sufficient policy support

Intervention must be strong enough and last long enough to alter research incentives.

2

Research is redirected

Innovation moves from dirty towards clean technologies.

3

The technology lead changes

Clean technologies catch up and eventually overtake dirty ones.

Why intervention can be temporary

The objective is to change the technological lead that shapes later innovation incentives. Once clean technology has established that lead, the same support need not continue forever.

Timing

Why delaying intervention is costly

Redirecting innovation does not instantly erase the initial dirty advantage. While clean technology is catching up, researchers are improving an input that is initially less productive, so productivity growth is low during the transition.

Delaying intervention leaves the economy on its laissez-faire path for longer. Dirty technology continues to advance, making the clean sector further behind when policy begins. A larger gap requires a longer intervention and extends the low-growth catch-up period.

Waiting makes intervention more costly because it increases the technological distance that clean innovation must recover.

Welfare

Consumption and environmental quality both matter

Private research compares monopoly profits, whereas the planner also values the environmental consequence of dirty production. A representative household values consumption and environmental quality. With no population growth, the planner maximises:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{u(C_t,S_t)}{(1+\rho)^t}$$

$u(C_t,S_t)$

Utility from consumption and environmental quality at date $t$.

Discounting

$(1+\rho)^t$ reduces the weight placed on utility at later dates.

Preferences

Utility is increasing and concave in both arguments: consumption and environmental quality are valued, with diminishing marginal utility.

The two boundary conditions make the environmental part of this objective precise:

$$\lim_{S\to0^+}u(C,S)=-\infty,\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial S}(C,\bar S)=0$$

As environmental quality approaches zero

Utility tends to minus infinity. Environmental collapse therefore has an extreme welfare cost in the model; environmental quality is critical to the household’s welfare.

At preindustrial environmental quality

The marginal utility of environmental quality is zero at $\bar S$. This condition concerns the additional utility from a marginal increase in environmental quality at that reference level.

First best

A carbon tax and clean-innovation subsidies

The first-best allocation is the allocation preferred by the planner who values both consumption and environmental quality. It can be implemented through private decisions using a Pigovian carbon tax, research subsidies for clean innovation and a subsidy to all machines.

Why several instruments are needed

The carbon tax addresses the pollution externality by making the environmental cost of dirty production relevant to firms’ production decisions.

The clean-research subsidy addresses the direction of innovation. A one-period monopoly reward does not include all the effects of an innovation on later technology and later research.

The subsidy to all machines addresses a separate distortion: monopoly supply of machines.

The implementation result is used here without a full welfare proof.

2 · Write equal profits using sector revenues

Use the research-reward expression above with sectors $P$ and $E$. The common factors in the two expressions cancel, as do the equal denominators:

$$\Pi_{Pt}=\Pi_{Et}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_Pp_{Pt}Y_{Pt}=h_Ep_{Et}Y_{Et}.$$
$$\frac{p_{Pt}Y_{Pt}}{p_{Et}Y_{Et}}=\frac{h_E}{h_P}. $$
3 · Express the energy share of total input income
$$\begin{aligned}\text{energy income share}&=\frac{p_{Et}Y_{Et}}{p_{Pt}Y_{Pt}+p_{Et}Y_{Et}}\\&=\frac{1}{\dfrac{p_{Pt}Y_{Pt}}{p_{Et}Y_{Et}}+1}\\&=\frac{1}{h_E/h_P+1}=\frac{h_P}{h_E+h_P}.\end{aligned}$$

Under the definitions used in this calculation, the numerator is $h_P$.

Results

Short-run emissions and the long-run transition

The immediate and long-run effects do not point in the same direction. The short run concerns the source of electricity used today; the long run concerns the technology that researchers improve for the future.

SHORT RUN AND LONG RUN DO NOT POINT IN THE SAME DIRECTION

Short run: gas replaces coal

Gas emits less CO₂ per unit of electricity than coal, so emissions fall.

Long run: clean innovation weakens

Lower clean-research incentives delay, and may prevent, the zero-carbon transition.

Welfare result

Without policy, the gas boom reduces welfare in the model. Correcting emissions and innovation incentives can reverse this result.
