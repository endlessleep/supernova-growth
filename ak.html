Chapter 2 — Economic Growth

Externalities and the “AK” Model

Why endogenous growth?

Motivation

Why explain technological progress?

In the Solow model, once the economy reaches its regular growth path, output per capita grows at the rate of exogenous technical progress, also referred to as TFP growth.

Reading the terms

“Exogenous” means that the model takes this rate as given: it does not explain the decisions that produce it. TFP means total factor productivity, the productivity component of production.

There are three reasons to connect productivity growth to economic decisions.

1

Firm innovation

Firms undertake industrial innovations because they expect profits.

2

Public research

Governments allocate public funds to research and development (R&D).

3

Capital accumulation

Finally, capital accumulation can contribute to productivity growth; this is the channel developed in the AK model below.

Why this matters

A complete growth theory therefore needs to describe how productivity growth is generated. This matters because, on the long-run path, productivity growth determines the growth of output per capita. Explaining that growth requires going beyond an assumed rate of technical progress.

Framework

The financing problem and two possible approaches

To make innovation endogenous, a model must describe the economic incentives that lead agents to innovate.

The financing problem

This raises a financing problem: if technology has constant returns to scale and each production factor is paid its marginal product, the Euler theorem implies that factor payments exhaust output. There is no remaining part of output to reward innovation separately under these assumptions.

01 · Production externalities

There are two approaches. The first uses externalities: production decisions affect productivity beyond the benefits taken into account by the individual decision-maker. This is the approach of the AK model studied here.

02 · Imperfect competition

The second allows imperfect competition in at least some sectors. Innovators can then obtain a rent, meaning earnings associated with their innovation that provide an incentive to undertake it. Romer’s variety-of-goods model and the Schumpeterian model of Aghion–Howitt are examples of this second approach.

The AK model

Production

Firms’ production and the capital externality

Two channels through which capital affects production

Capital enters the model twice: as an input used by a firm, and through the common productivity level. The two equations describe these different roles.

1 · Direct input: the firm’s own capital

$$y_i=\bar A k_i^\alpha L_i^{1-\alpha}$$

Here $\alpha$ is the exponent on the firm’s own capital. With $0<\alpha<1$, capital has decreasing marginal returns when $\bar A$ and labour are held fixed. The exponent $1-\alpha$ is the labour exponent.

2 · External effect: the economy’s total capital

$$\bar A=A_0\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_i\right)^\eta$$

Here $\eta$ is the exponent on total capital in the productivity equation. It measures the strength of the capital externality. $A_0$ sets the baseline productivity coefficient; $\bar A$ is the resulting productivity level, which depends on aggregate capital.

Firm-level inputs

The index $i$ identifies a firm. Its output is $y_i$, its capital is $k_i$, and its labour input is $L_i$.

Where the externality enters

The term $\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_i$ is the capital stock of all $N$ firms together. Productivity therefore depends on aggregate capital, rather than only on a firm’s own input. The parameter $\eta$ measures the size of this externality: it governs how aggregate capital enters the productivity term.

The firm’s perspective

An individual firm treats $\bar A$ as given when making its decision. The externality is precisely the productivity effect of aggregate accumulation that is not internalized in this private decision. This distinction explains why the private and aggregate returns to capital can differ.

Aggregation

From firms’ production to the AK form

$$K=\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_i,\qquad Y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_i$$

Aggregate quantities

Total capital $K$ and total output $Y$ are the sums of firm-level quantities.

Labour normalization

Labour is normalized by setting $L_i=1$. Total labour is therefore $L=N$.

Identical firms

All firms are assumed identical, so each holds the same capital stock $k_i=K/N$.

Substitute the identical-firm assumptions

With $L_i=1$, each firm produces $A_0K^\eta(K/N)^\alpha$. There are $N$ identical firms, so multiplying this expression by $N$ gives total output:

$$Y=N\times A_0K^\eta\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)^\alpha$$
$$Y=A_0N^{1-\alpha}K^{\alpha+\eta}$$

The factor $N^{1-\alpha}$ comes from $N/N^\alpha$. The exponent $\alpha+\eta$ comes from multiplying $K^\alpha$ by $K^\eta$: powers of the same base have their exponents added.

Read the capital exponent

In the aggregate formula, the exponent on capital is $\alpha+\eta$. It combines capital’s role as a direct production input, represented by $\alpha$, with its role in the common productivity term, represented by $\eta$. Holding $N$ fixed, there are three cases:

$\alpha+\eta<1$ — Decreasing marginal returns.

As aggregate capital rises, each additional unit contributes less additional output.

$\alpha+\eta>1$ — Increasing marginal returns.

As aggregate capital rises, each additional unit contributes more additional output.

$\alpha+\eta=1$ — Constant marginal returns.

Each additional unit of aggregate capital contributes the same additional output. This is the AK case.

Why is it called “AK”?

The AK model is the case in which output is proportional to capital. In the aggregate production function above, this requires the exponent on $K$ to equal one:

$$\alpha+\eta=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y=\underbrace{A_0N^{1-\alpha}}_{\text{constant coefficient}}K$$

The name “AK” refers to this form: a constant productivity coefficient multiplying capital $K$. In this chapter, that coefficient is written explicitly as $A_0N^{1-\alpha}$, with $N$ fixed. It is not the same object as the firm’s $\bar A$, which depends on total capital.

Why impose $\alpha+\eta=1$?

This is a specific assumption, not a rule that always holds. It selects the case where the external productivity effect exactly offsets decreasing marginal returns at firm level. The condition links the two exponents: $\eta=1-\alpha$. It does not mean that $\alpha$ itself equals one.

Optimization

Individual optimization and consumption growth

The household chooses its consumption path $c(t)$ to maximize discounted utility. Its capital accumulation equation is production minus consumption.

Variables in this chapter

Here $k$ denotes the capital of the representative firm/household, so $K=Nk$ in the symmetric economy; $N$ is fixed in the growth expressions.

$$\max_{\{c(t)\}}\int_0^\infty u(c(t))e^{-\rho t}\,dt,\qquad \frac{dk}{dt}=\bar A k^\alpha-c$$
$$u(c)=\frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}$$

Preferences

The parameter $\rho$ is the rate of time preference, and $\varepsilon$ is the utility-curvature parameter; the intertemporal elasticity of substitution is $1/\varepsilon$.

The specified setting

The displayed utility expression is written for $\varepsilon\ne1$. The accumulation equation here has no depreciation or population-growth term.

Applying the Ramsey optimization conditions to this constraint gives the following consumption-growth expression. The derivation is available below.

$$g_c\equiv\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=\frac{\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\alpha A_0N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
1
Set up the private problem.

Treat $\bar A$ as given when differentiating with respect to private capital. With $\lambda$ denoting the current-value multiplier, the current-value Hamiltonian is:

$$\mathcal H=u(c)+\lambda(\bar A k^\alpha-c)$$
2
Use the consumption and capital conditions.

First-order condition on consumption: $\partial\mathcal H/\partial c=0 \implies \lambda=u'(c)=c^{-\varepsilon}$. Differentiating with respect to time gives $\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = -\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}\dfrac{dc(t)}{dt}$, so dividing by $\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon g_c}$$

Differentiating $\mathcal H$ with respect to private capital $k$, treating $\bar A$ as given: $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\lambda\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$. Apply the current-value costate equation:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\rho\lambda-\lambda\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}}$$

Dividing by $\lambda$ gives:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho-\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}}$$
3
Rearrange, then impose symmetry.

Equating the two expressions for $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}$ gives $-\varepsilon g_c = \rho - \alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$, which rearranges to $g_c = \dfrac{\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$. Only after taking this private derivative do we substitute $\bar A=A_0(Nk)^\eta$:

$$\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}=\alpha A_0N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$$

This yields the displayed formula. Differentiating the aggregate productivity effect as part of the private return would change the problem: households do not internalize it.

Why set aside increasing returns?

Why set aside $\alpha+\eta>1$? In this case, the exponent $\alpha+\eta-1$ in the growth formula is positive. As capital accumulates, the return term rises and consumption growth accelerates instead of remaining constant. This creates an explosive-growth concern and does not deliver the constant-growth case pursued in the chapter. This argument motivates the restriction; it does not establish that every possible path must explode.

Growth

The growth of the decentralized economy

The AK case imposes $\alpha+\eta=1$. The capital exponent in the consumption-growth formula becomes zero, so the growth rate no longer changes with the level of capital.

$$g=g_c=\frac{\alpha A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
1
Substitute the AK restriction.
$$k^{\alpha+\eta-1}=k^0=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_c=\frac{\alpha A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
2
Read this as the economy’s balanced growth rate.

In this case, output per person is $y=A_0N^\eta k$. On the balanced growth path, consumption and capital grow together, so output has the same growth rate. With fixed $N$, aggregate and per-person growth rates also coincide. This is the economy’s common growth rate $g$.

What determines growth?

The numerator compares the private return $\alpha A_0N^\eta$ with impatience $\rho$.

Productivity · $A_0$

A larger baseline productivity $A_0$ raises growth.

Economy size · $N$

With positive externalities, a larger $N$ raises the return through $N^\eta$.

Impatience · $\rho$

Greater impatience lowers growth.

Consumption response · $1/\varepsilon$

The factor $1/\varepsilon$ determines how strongly consumption growth responds to the return–impatience difference; when that difference is positive, a larger $\varepsilon$ lowers the positive growth rate.

The AK restriction

The parameters $\alpha$ and $\eta$ must satisfy $\alpha+\eta=1$ in this case.

No automatic catch-up

Initial capital does not appear in this growth-rate expression. A country does not grow faster simply because it starts with less capital. Countries with the same parameters have the same growth rate, rather than a catch-up mechanism driven by their initial capital gap. This is what “no cross-country convergence” means in the AK model.

Private incentives and public intervention

Planner

Growth in the centralized economy

The planner’s perspective

A benevolent central planner chooses consumption and investment for the economy as a whole. Unlike the private decision-maker, the planner takes account of all production externalities: aggregate accumulation changes both the direct capital input and the common productivity term.

$$\frac{dK}{dt}=A_0N^{1-\alpha}K^{\alpha+\eta}-C$$

Here $C$ is total consumption. The resources not consumed become additional aggregate capital. The corresponding growth expression is:

$$g^*=\frac{A_0(\alpha+\eta)N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

The star marks the centralized outcome, and $k=K/N$ as before.

Social return versus private return

The return coefficient is now $\alpha+\eta$, whereas private agents take account only of $\alpha$. The additional term reflects the external productivity effect included by the planner.

Before imposing the AK restriction, this expression depends on $k$ and need not be constant.

In the AK case, $\alpha+\eta=1$, the centralized growth rate is:

$$g^*=\frac{A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}>g$$

Use per-person quantities $k=K/N$ and $c=C/N$, with fixed $N$, to compare the two problems in the same notation.

1
Decentralized Hamiltonian
$$\mathcal H^D=u(c)+\lambda\left[\bar A k^\alpha-c\right]$$

The private agent treats $\bar A$ as given when differentiating with respect to $k$. Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H^D}{\partial k}=\lambda\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$$

The coefficient in the private return to capital contains only $\alpha$.

2
Centralized Hamiltonian

The planner internalizes the capital externality, so use:

$$\mathcal H^C=u(c)+\lambda\left[A_0N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta}-c\right]$$

Now differentiate with respect to $k$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H^C}{\partial k}=\lambda A_0N^\eta(\alpha+\eta)k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$$

The coefficient in the social return contains $\alpha+\eta$. This is the key difference.

3
Insert this return into the Ramsey consumption-growth equation

From the costate equation $\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal H^C}{\partial k}=\rho\lambda-\lambda A_0N^\eta(\alpha+\eta)k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$, dividing by $\lambda$ yields $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho-A_0N^\eta(\alpha+\eta)k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$. Combining with $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\dfrac{1}{c}\dfrac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon g^*$:

$$g^*=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[A_0N^\eta(\alpha+\eta)k^{\alpha+\eta-1}-\rho\right]$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{g^*=\frac{A_0(\alpha+\eta)N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

In the AK case, $\alpha+\eta=1$, so $k^{\alpha+\eta-1}=1$:

$$\boxed{g^*=\frac{A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

Key intuition

The decentralized agent sees only the direct effect of capital, $\alpha$. The centralized planner also internalizes the positive productivity externality, $\eta$.

$$\underbrace{\alpha}_{\text{private return coefficient}}\qquad\underbrace{\alpha+\eta}_{\text{social return coefficient}}$$

Why policy enters the model

With the positive externality considered here, the planner values investment more highly and chooses a higher growth rate. The difference between the two outcomes motivates the next question: can taxes and subsidies make private incentives reflect the return taken into account by the planner?

⚠ Important: when do we substitute $\bar A$?

The two Hamiltonians look almost the same, but the order of operations differs fundamentally.

Private agent — $\bar A$ is taken as given (not a function of own $k$), so differentiate first:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal H^D}{\partial k}=\lambda\,\alpha\bar A\, k^{\alpha-1}$$

Only after taking this derivative, impose symmetry and substitute $\bar A=A_0(Nk)^\eta$:

$$\alpha\bar A\, k^{\alpha-1}=\alpha A_0 N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Private: differentiate first, substitute }\bar A\text{ after}}$$

Reason: the private agent does not internalize the effect of its own capital on aggregate productivity.

Planner — the externality is internalized, so write the full per-person term $A_0 N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta}$ directly, then differentiate:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal H^C}{\partial k}=\lambda\, A_0 N^\eta(\alpha+\eta)\,k^{\alpha+\eta-1}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Planner: include full productivity effect first, then differentiate}}$$

Reason: the planner accounts for both the direct effect of capital ($\alpha$) and the external productivity effect ($\eta$). Hence the private return coefficient is $\alpha$, while the social return coefficient is $\alpha+\eta$.

Policy

Taxes and subsidies: implementing centralized growth

Find a system of taxes and subsidies that makes decentralized growth equal centralized growth. Each household pays a lump-sum tax $T$, and each producer receives a subsidy $\tau y = \tau f(k)$, proportional to its output.

Notation convention — Current-value multiplier $\lambda$ everywhere

This entire course systematically uses the current-value Hamiltonian $\mathcal H$ and the current-value multiplier $\lambda$. In all Hamiltonian derivations, the dynamic costate equation is:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = \rho\lambda - \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}}$$

The discount-rate term $\rho\lambda$ remains explicit in the costate equation. We do not switch to a present-value multiplier $\mu$ or a present-value costate equation. Using $\boxed{\lambda\text{ everywhere}}$ ensures complete consistency across the Ramsey and AK chapters.

1
Private capital accumulation and current-value Hamiltonian

The producer receives $(1+\tau)y$. Consumption and the lump-sum tax use $c+T$, giving the private capital accumulation equation:

$$\frac{dk(t)}{dt}=(1+\tau)\bar A k^\alpha-c-T$$

The private household treats both $\bar A$ and the lump-sum tax $T$ as given. With current-value multiplier $\lambda$, the Hamiltonian is:

$$\boxed{\mathcal H=u(c)+\lambda\left[(1+\tau)\bar A k^\alpha-c-T\right]}$$

Lump-sum tax $T$

$T$ is a fixed lump-sum payment for the individual, not a tax per unit of capital or consumption (we write $T$ to avoid confusion with time $t$). The government balances its budget in aggregate via $NT=\tau Y \implies T=\tau y=\tau\bar A k^\alpha$, but the private household takes $T$ as given and does not substitute this budget identity before taking the private derivative.

2
Step 1 — FOC with respect to consumption

Since $c$ is a control variable, set the derivative of $\mathcal H$ with respect to $c$ to zero:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial c}=0$$

With CRRA utility, $u'(c)=c^{-\varepsilon}$, therefore:

$$\boxed{\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}}$$
3
Step 2 — Dynamic condition for capital

Use the current-value costate equation:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}}$$

Because the household treats $\bar A$, $T$, and $\tau$ as given, differentiate $\mathcal H$ with respect to $k$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\lambda(1+\tau)\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$$

Substituting into the costate equation:

$$\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\lambda(1+\tau)\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$$

Divide by $\lambda$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho-(1+\tau)\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}}$$
4
Step 3 — Differentiate $\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}$ with respect to time

Since $\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}$, differentiate with respect to time:

$$\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$

Divide by $\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}$ to obtain:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon g_c}$$
5
Step 4 — Equate the two expressions and isolate $g_c$

Therefore:

$$-\varepsilon g_c=\rho-(1+\tau)\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}$$

Rearrange:

$$\boxed{g_c=\frac{(1+\tau)\alpha\bar A k^{\alpha-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

Only AFTER deriving the private marginal return should we substitute the externality $\bar A=A_0(Nk)^\eta$:

$$g_c=\frac{(1+\tau)\alpha A_0N^\eta k^{\alpha+\eta-1}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

Then impose the AK condition $\alpha+\eta=1$, so $k^{\alpha+\eta-1}=k^0=1$, and obtain:

$$\boxed{g_\tau=\frac{(1+\tau)\alpha A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$
6
Step 5 — Policy calculation and balanced budget

The planner targets $g_\tau = g^*$, where $g^*=\dfrac{A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}$:

$$\frac{(1+\tau^*)\alpha A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}=\frac{A_0N^\eta-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

This implies:

$$(1+\tau^*)\alpha=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1+\tau^*=\frac{1}{\alpha}$$

and therefore:

$$\boxed{\tau^*=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}=\frac{\eta}{\alpha}>0}$$

The lump-sum tax balances the public budget in aggregate ($NT=\tau Y$):

$$\boxed{T^*=\tau^* y=\tau^*\bar A k^\alpha=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bar A k^\alpha}$$

The output subsidy corrects the gap between the private return ($\alpha$) and the social return ($\alpha+\eta=1$), while the lump-sum tax $T$ finances the subsidy without distorting capital accumulation decisions.

Key takeaways

The AK model: essential results

At the firm level

The model is based on production externalities. At the level of an individual firm, capital has decreasing marginal returns: holding the productivity environment fixed, successive additions to the firm’s capital generate smaller additions to its output.

At the economy level

At the aggregate level, capital accumulation also improves that productivity environment. Under the AK restriction $\alpha+\eta=1$, this external effect exactly offsets decreasing marginal returns at the firm level. Aggregate capital then has constant marginal returns, removing the source of declining growth emphasized in the Solow model.

Growth and policy

The AK model delivers a constant growth rate and no cross-country convergence. The decentralized equilibrium also performs less well than the centralized equilibrium, which takes externalities into account. This leaves a role for public intervention through taxes and subsidies.

Public spending and growth

Extension

Public spending as a production externality

The extension changes the source of the production externality. Output per capita now depends on private capital per capita $k$ and government expenditure per capita $\gamma$:

$$y=Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}$$

The productive role of public expenditure

The coefficient $A$ describes productivity in this extension. Public expenditure enters production with exponent $1-\alpha$. An individual takes $\gamma$ as given when choosing consumption and capital, even though public expenditure is financed by taxes on production.

$$\gamma=\tau y,\qquad u(c)=\frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}$$

Financing and the allocation problem

The government taxes a fraction $\tau$ of production and spends the resulting revenue. Thus $\gamma=\tau y$ is the balanced-budget condition, expressed per capita. Preferences retain the constant intertemporal elasticity $1/\varepsilon$. The exercise compares the growth rate chosen privately with the one achieved by a planner who accounts for the productive role and resource cost of public spending.

✎ Application

Public spending — exercise and worked solutions

Use the production function, balanced budget and preferences above to compare private decisions with the planner’s allocation.

  1. 1.Write the capital accumulation equation in the decentralized economy, where individuals take $\gamma$ as given and do not see the link between their taxes and public expenditure.
  2. 2.What is the growth rate of consumption? Show that it depends on $\gamma/k$.
  3. 3.Is there a level of $\gamma/k$ that maximizes the decentralized growth rate?
  4. 4.Write the benevolent central planner’s capital accumulation equation and programme.
  5. 5.What growth rate does the central planner achieve? Compare it with the maximum decentralized growth rate and comment.

Method and notation

These solutions follow the course convention: current-value Hamiltonian $\mathcal H$, current-value multiplier $\lambda$, and dynamic costate equation $\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = \rho\lambda - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}$. Here $\tau$ is a tax rate on output (not the subsidy rate of the previous section). $g_c^d$ and $g_c^c$ denote decentralized and centralized consumption growth; a star indicates the maximum over the tax rate.

Individuals take $\gamma$ as given. They therefore do not replace it by $\tau y$ before solving their own optimization problem. Each unit of production is taxed at rate $\tau$, so output available after tax is $(1-\tau)y$.

$$\boxed{\frac{dk(t)}{dt}=(1-\tau)Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}-c}$$

Capital accumulation is after-tax output minus consumption. The government budget will be used later to relate the equilibrium spending ratio to the tax rate.

1
Write the current-value Hamiltonian and consumption condition

With $y=Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}$, the household treats public expenditure $\gamma$ and the tax rate $\tau$ as given. The current-value Hamiltonian is:

$$\mathcal H=u(c)+\lambda\left[(1-\tau)Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}-c\right]$$

The first-order condition with respect to consumption is $\partial\mathcal H/\partial c=0$. With CRRA utility $u'(c)=c^{-\varepsilon}$:

$$\boxed{\lambda=u'(c)=c^{-\varepsilon}}$$

Differentiating with respect to time gives $\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = -\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}\dfrac{dc(t)}{dt}$. Dividing by $\lambda=c^{-\varepsilon}$ gives:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon g_c^d}$$
2
Dynamic condition for capital

Differentiating $\mathcal H$ with respect to private capital $k$, holding $\gamma$ fixed:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\lambda(1-\tau)\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}$$

Use the dynamic costate equation:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\rho\lambda-\lambda(1-\tau)\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}}$$

Divide by $\lambda$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho-(1-\tau)\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}}$$
3
Equate the expressions and isolate consumption growth

Equating the two expressions for $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}$:

$$-\varepsilon g_c^d=\rho-(1-\tau)\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}$$

Rearranging:

$$\boxed{g_c^d=\frac{(1-\tau)\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

Using $k^{\alpha-1}\gamma^{1-\alpha}=\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}$, we obtain:

$$\boxed{g_c^d=\frac{(1-\tau)\alpha A\left(\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

The return depends on public expenditure relative to capital, $\gamma/k$, and on the fraction $1-\tau$ retained after tax.

1
Distinguish positive growth from maximum growth.

The inequality $(1-\tau)\alpha A(\gamma/k)^{1-\alpha}>\rho$ makes $g_c^d$ positive. It does not identify the spending ratio that maximizes this growth rate.

Precision

The supplied correction calls this a “stability” condition. The displayed inequality establishes positive consumption growth; by itself, it is not a stability condition.

2
Use budget balance to link the spending ratio and the tax rate.

Now impose $\gamma=\tau y$ at equilibrium, using $y=Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}$:

$$\frac{\gamma}{k}=\frac{\tau y}{k}=\frac{\tau Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}}{k}=\tau A\left(\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}$$

For a positive spending ratio, divide by $(\gamma/k)^{1-\alpha}$:

$$\left(\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)^\alpha=\tau A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\gamma}{k}=(\tau A)^{1/\alpha}$$
3
Express decentralized growth as a function of the tax rate.
$$g_c^d=\frac{(1-\tau)\alpha A\left((\tau A)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
$$g_c^d=\frac{(1-\tau)\alpha A(\tau A)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

Collect the powers of $A$: $A\times A^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}=A^{1/\alpha}$. Hence:

$$g_c^d(\tau)=\frac{(1-\tau)\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}\alpha A^{1/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
4
Maximize using the logarithm, as in the correction.

The factors $\alpha A^{1/\alpha}$ and $\varepsilon$ are positive, and $\rho$ is constant. Maximizing growth is therefore equivalent to maximizing $f(\tau)=(1-\tau)\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$. For $0<\tau<1$, its logarithm is:

$$\ln f(\tau)=\ln(1-\tau)+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\ln\tau$$

Because the logarithm is increasing, it preserves the maximizing tax rate. Differentiate and set the result equal to zero:

$$-\frac{1}{1-\tau}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\tau}=0$$
$$\frac{1}{1-\tau}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha\tau}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}$$
$$\alpha\tau=(1-\alpha)(1-\tau)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\tau^*=1-\alpha}$$

Substitute this rate into the spending-ratio formula:

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)^*=\left[(1-\alpha)A\right]^{1/\alpha}}$$
5
Evaluate maximum decentralized growth.

At $\tau^*=1-\alpha$, the after-tax share $1-\tau^*$ equals $\alpha$. This supplies the second factor of $\alpha$ in the numerator:

$$g_c^{d*}=\frac{[1-(1-\alpha)](1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}\alpha A^{1/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
$$\boxed{g_c^{d*}=\frac{\alpha^2(1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}A^{1/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$
1
Internalize the government budget in production.

The planner takes account of the link $\gamma=\tau y$ when describing the economy’s production. Substitute it before calculating the aggregate return:

$$y=Ak^\alpha\gamma^{1-\alpha}=Ak^\alpha(\tau y)^{1-\alpha}$$

Divide by $y^{1-\alpha}$, then raise both sides to the power $1/\alpha$:

$$y^\alpha=A\tau^{1-\alpha}k^\alpha$$
$$y=A^{1/\alpha}\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}k=\tilde A k$$
$$\tilde A=A^{1/\alpha}\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$$

The symbol $\tilde A$ is the coefficient defined in the correction. For a given tax rate, production is proportional to capital.

2
Write the resource constraint and programme.

The fraction $\tau$ of output finances public expenditure. The remaining output is consumed or invested:

$$\frac{dk(t)}{dt}=(1-\tau)y-c=(1-\tau)\tilde A k-c$$
$$\max_{\{c\}}\int_0^\infty\frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$

The programme is subject to this accumulation equation and the given initial capital stock. As in the correction, first obtain growth for a given $\tau$, then choose the rate that maximizes it.

1
Use the planner’s current-value Hamiltonian

With aggregate production linear in capital, $y=\tilde A k$, the planner’s current-value Hamiltonian is:

$$\mathcal H=u(c)+\lambda\left[(1-\tau)\tilde A k-c\right]$$

The FOC with respect to consumption yields $\lambda=u'(c)=c^{-\varepsilon}$, so differentiating with respect to time and dividing by $\lambda$ gives:

$$\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon g_c^c$$

Differentiating $\mathcal H$ with respect to capital $k$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\lambda(1-\tau)\tilde A$$

Apply the dynamic costate equation:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho\lambda-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial k}=\rho\lambda-\lambda(1-\tau)\tilde A}$$

Divide by $\lambda$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}=\rho-(1-\tau)\tilde A}$$

Equating $-\varepsilon g_c^c=\rho-(1-\tau)\tilde A$ yields:

$$\varepsilon g_c^c+\rho=(1-\tau)\tilde A$$
2
Obtain centralized growth for a given tax rate
$$g_c^c=\frac{(1-\tau)\tilde A-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

Substitute $\tilde A=A^{1/\alpha}\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{g_c^c(\tau)=\frac{(1-\tau)A^{1/\alpha}\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

Compare this with decentralized growth at the same tax rate:

$$g_c^d(\tau)=\frac{(1-\tau)\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}\alpha A^{1/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

The decentralized return includes the extra factor $\alpha<1$. For the same interior tax rate, the planner’s growth rate is therefore higher: the planner accounts for the link between public spending and production.

3
Compare with the maximum decentralized growth rate

The correction subtracts the maximum decentralized rate from the centralized rate:

$$g_c^c(\tau)-g_c^{d*}=\frac{A^{1/\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\left[(1-\tau)\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}-\alpha^2(1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}\right]$$

To maximize centralized growth, maximize the same function $f(\tau)=(1-\tau)\tau^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$ used in question 3. Its maximizing rate is therefore again $\tau^*=1-\alpha$. At this rate:

$$g_c^{c*}=\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}A^{1/\alpha}-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

The comparison reduces to:

$$\alpha(1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}>\alpha^2(1-\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$$

This holds because $0<\alpha<1$. Consequently:

$$\boxed{g_c^{c*}>g_c^{d*}}$$

Scope of the comparison

The supplied correction’s final “always higher” statement applies here at the maximizing tax rate $\tau^*=1-\alpha$. The centralized rate at an arbitrary tax rate need not exceed the maximum decentralized rate.

What to retain

Both growth rates are maximized at $ au^*=1-\alpha$, but their maxima differ. Private agents hold $\gamma$ fixed when differentiating; the planner first internalizes $\gamma=\tau y$. This difference in the return to capital explains why maximum centralized growth is higher.
