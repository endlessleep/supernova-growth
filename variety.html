Chapter 3 — Innovation and Growth

Growth in a model with variety of intermediate goods

Introduction

Context

From the AK model to variety of goods

In the AK model, positive growth comes from externalities that exactly offset the diminishing marginal return to capital at the firm level.

At the aggregate level, the marginal return to capital is constant: it does not depend on $K$. Growth therefore does not become exhausted in the way it does in the neo-classical growth model.

$$\eta+\alpha=1$$

However, this result relies on the strong assumption $(\eta+\alpha=1)$.

In the model with a variety of goods, long-run economic growth comes from R&D efforts that increase the number of intermediate goods available to produce the final good.

The comparison is therefore precise. The AK model obtains sustained growth through its aggregate return to capital; this model instead follows the creation of new intermediate-good varieties through research.

A model with several production sectors

A variety-of-goods model is more complex because it must describe more than one production sector and the links between them.

1

Growth is driven by technological change arising from deliberate investment decisions by profit-maximizing agents.

2

Technology is neither a conventional good nor a public good. In this model, it is a nonrival and partially excludable input.

3

Because a nonrival good introduces nonconvexity, price-taking competition cannot support the equilibrium. The model therefore uses monopolistic competition.

The main conclusions

The stock of human capital determines the growth rate. In equilibrium, too little human capital is devoted to research. Integration into world markets raises growth rates, whereas a large population alone is not sufficient to generate growth.

Framework

Building blocks of the model

This is a simplified version of the model: there are no capital goods, and the share of labour in the research sector is constant.

The economy contains three production sectors:

Final output sectoruses intermediate goods and labor; output: final good; perfect competition
→
Intermediate good sectorinput: final good; output: intermediate goods; monopolistic competition
→
Research sectordevelops new variety of intermediate goods; uses labor; perfect competition

This is the model’s production chain. The final-good sector combines labour with intermediate inputs. The intermediate-good sector uses final goods to supply those inputs, and the research sector uses labour to create new varieties. Each sector has a distinct role before the model determines growth.

Household sector

There is one representative household. It maximises an intertemporal utility function, and labour has no disutility in that function.

Households and production

Households

Maximisation program

$$\mathrm{Max}=\int_0^\infty \frac{c(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}dt$$

The household chooses its consumption path $c(t)$ over time. The programme evaluates consumption at every date and discounts future utility through the term $e^{-\rho t}$. The parameter $\varepsilon$ appears in the utility function, while $\rho$ is the discount rate.

Wealth accumulation equation:

$$\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=r(t)S(t)+w(t)L(t)-c(t)$$

This equation records the household’s wealth $S(t)$. Its change combines interest income $r(t)S(t)$ and labour income $w(t)L(t)$, minus current consumption $c(t)$.

Labour $L$ is constant: there is no population growth and no labour disutility in the utility function. The household takes the wage $w(t)$ and interest rate $r(t)$ as given.

We consider a constant interest rate, $r(t)=r$. A time-varying rate could be considered, but the resolution of this model leads to a constant $r$.

The transversality condition

$$\lim_{t\to\infty} S(t)e^{-rt}=0$$

On the optimal growth path

$$g_C=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

The course states this consumption-growth result for the optimal path. With the constant interest rate used here, it relates consumption growth to $r$, the discount rate $\rho$, and $\varepsilon$.

Final good sector

Production function

$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$

Here, $Y_t$ is value added in the final-good sector and $x_i$ is the quantity of intermediate input $i$ used by that sector.

The index $i$ runs from $0$ to $M_t$. Thus, $M_t$ measures the variety of intermediate inputs: a higher $M_t$ means that more distinct intermediate goods are available.

$L_1$ is the fixed quantity of labour employed in the final-good sector.

The integral collects the contribution of every available intermediate input. Final-good production therefore combines the fixed labour input $L_1$ with the full set of varieties available at date $t$.

Intermediate good sector

Production and GDP

Producing one unit of an intermediate input requires one unit of the final good. The quantity used for variety $i$ is therefore also the final-good requirement associated with that variety.

$$x_i=x_i$$

$X_t$ is the total amount of final good required to produce all intermediate goods.

$$X_t=\int_0^{M_t}x_i\,di$$

This integral adds the quantity used to produce every intermediate input. It is the total amount of final good absorbed by intermediate-good production.

GDP

$$GDP_t=Y_t-X_t$$

The final-good sector produces $Y_t$, but intermediate-good production uses $X_t$ units of that final good. GDP is therefore final-sector value added minus the final goods required to make the intermediate inputs.

and (because there is no capital good, so no investment)

$$C_t=GDP_t$$

There are no capital goods in this simplified model, so there is no investment term. The whole of GDP is therefore consumed: $C_t=GDP_t$. It follows that consumption and output have the same growth rate, $g_C=g_Y$.

and

$$g_C=g_Y$$
Perfect competition

Price of intermediate input and wages

Perfect competition in the final good sector ⇒ prices of labor and intermediate input are equal to their marginal productivity.

$$p_i=L_1^{1-\alpha}\alpha x_i^{\alpha-1}$$

For intermediate input $i$, this marginal-productivity rule gives the price $p_i$. The final good is the numeraire, so its price is 1 and $p_i$ is expressed in units of the final good.

$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$

The same rule gives the wage $w_t$: the displayed expression is the marginal productivity of labour in the final-good sector, obtained from the production function above.

Intermediate goods and variety

Monopolistic competition

Profit maximisation in the intermediate good sector

Monopolistic competition ⇒ each firm chooses the quantity $x_i$ that, at the given price $p_i$, maximises its profit

$$\Pi_i=p_ix_i-x_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha-x_i$$

Profit is revenue, $p_ix_i$, minus the final good required to produce the intermediate input, $x_i$. The second expression is obtained by substituting the price $p_i$ derived in the final-good sector into the profit equation.

Profit maximisation with respect to $x_i$ leads to

$$x_i=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$
1 · Substitute the price equation

From $p_i=L_1^{1-\alpha}\alpha x_i^{\alpha-1}$, revenue is $p_ix_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha$. Therefore $\Pi_i=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^\alpha-x_i$.

2 · Maximise with respect to $x_i$
$$\frac{d\Pi_i}{dx_i}=\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^{\alpha-1}-1=0$$

The first-order condition sets the marginal change in profit equal to zero.

3 · Isolate $x_i$
$$\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}x_i^{\alpha-1}=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_i^{1-\alpha}=\alpha^2L_1^{1-\alpha}$$
$$x_i=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

Profit of firm $i$

$$\Pi_i=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

This profit does not depend on $i$. Every firm therefore produces the same quantity and uses the same amount of final good as intermediate consumption. With $M_t$ identical varieties, total intermediate production is divided equally across them:

$$x_i=\frac{X_t}{M_t}=x$$
Variety

Economic growth and the variety of intermediate inputs

$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$
$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}(X_t/M_t)^\alpha di$$
$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t^{1-\alpha}X_t^\alpha$$

All varieties are used in the same quantity, so $x_i=X_t/M_t$. Substituting this common quantity makes the integrand constant over the interval from $0$ to $M_t$. Integrating it therefore introduces the factor $M_t$, which yields the final expression above.

For a given value of $X_t$, $Y_t$ is an increasing function of $M_t$.

1 · Integrate the common quantity
$$Y_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t\left(\frac{X_t}{M_t}\right)^\alpha=L_1^{1-\alpha}M_t^{1-\alpha}X_t^\alpha$$
2 · Use $X_t=M_tx$
$$Y_t=M_t^{1-\alpha}L_1^{1-\alpha}(M_tx)^\alpha=M_tL_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha$$
3 · Obtain GDP

Because total intermediate-good production is $X_t=M_tx$, subtracting it from $Y_t$ gives:

$$GDP_t=M_t\left(L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x\right)$$

The common factor $M_t$ appears in both output and GDP. Since $C_t=GDP_t$, the professor’s conclusion is that $Y$, $GDP$, and $C$ grow at the same rate as the measure of intermediate-good variety $M_t$.

Growth and variety

$Y_t$, $GDP_t$, and $C_t$ all grow at the same rate as the measure of intermediate-good variety, $M_t$.

Research sector

New varieties of intermediate goods

Assumption: growth in $M_t$ depends on R&D effort, where $L_2$ is the amount of labour in the research sector.

$$\frac{dM_t}{dt}=\lambda M_tL_2$$

Here, $M_t$ is the existing number of varieties, $L_2$ is labour devoted to research, and $\lambda$ is research productivity. The left-hand side is the flow of new varieties. The equation states that this flow is generated by existing varieties and research labour. The course notes that research creates a positive externality.

Research, interest rate and growth

Research sector

Profit in the research sector

Each new intermediate good generates a flow of profit per unit of time:

$$\Pi=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

The value of each innovation is the discounted sum of profits it can generate:

$$\frac{\Pi}{r}$$

A new variety gives its producer a profit flow $\Pi$. It therefore has a value. With the constant profit flow and interest rate used in the course, the value of that innovation is written $\Pi/r$. The research sector uses this value when it creates new varieties.

The profit in the research sector is:

$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\frac{dM_t}{dt}-w_tL_2$$
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2$$

The first term is the value generated by the flow of innovations, $dM_t/dt$; the second term is the wage cost of research labour. The next line substitutes the research equation already established in the course.

The research sector is in perfect competition with free entry, so its profit is null. This condition links the return $r$ to the value of innovation and the research wage:

$$r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t$$
1 · Substitute the innovation flow
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\frac{dM_t}{dt}-w_tL_2=\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2$$
2 · Apply zero profit
$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_tL_2-w_tL_2=0$$

For research labour $L_2$, divide by $L_2$ and rearrange:

$$\frac{\Pi}{r}\lambda M_t=w_t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r=\frac{\lambda M_t\Pi}{w_t}$$
Interest rate

Interest rate

To make the research-arbitrage condition usable, the course substitutes the wage and profit expressions already obtained.

$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di$$
$$x_i=\frac{X_t}{M_t}=x$$
$$x=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$$

Because all intermediate inputs have the same quantity $x$, the wage integral contains the same term across $M_t$ varieties. Substituting $x=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$ gives

$$w_t=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$$

Substituting this wage and the profit flow $\Pi$ into the research-arbitrage condition makes the common terms cancel. Hence

$$r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t=\alpha\lambda L_1$$
1 · Substitute the common intermediate quantity into the wage
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}M_tx^\alpha$$
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L_1^{-\alpha}M_t\left(L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}\right)^\alpha=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$$
2 · Substitute profit and wage into the arbitrage condition
$$r=\lambda M_t\frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t}$$

The factors $M_t$ and $1-\alpha$ cancel. Combining the remaining powers of $\alpha$ gives:

$$r=\alpha\lambda L_1$$
Growth rate

The growth rate of the economy

Up to now, the course has obtained one expression for growth from research, one for consumption growth, and one for the interest rate. They are combined because GDP and consumption grow at the same rate in this model.

GDP grows at the same rate as $M_t$
$$g=\lambda L_2$$

Since growth in varieties is $\lambda M_tL_2$, the growth rate of the variety stock is $\lambda L_2$. Output, GDP, and consumption grow at this same rate.

The growth rate of consumption is
$$g_C=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

This is the optimal consumption-growth result from the household sector.

The interest rate is
$$r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t=\alpha\lambda L_1$$

This is the research-arbitrage result after substituting the wage and profit expressions.

Using labour-market equilibrium,

$$L_1+L_2=L$$

These relationships determine how total labour is divided between final-good production and research. Combining them leads to

$$\boxed{g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}}$$
1 · Equate GDP and consumption growth
$$g=g_C=\frac{\alpha\lambda L_1-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
2 · Use research growth and labour-market equilibrium
$$g=\lambda L_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_2=\frac{g}{\lambda}$$
$$L_1=L-L_2=L-\frac{g}{\lambda}$$
3 · Substitute $L_1$
$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda\left(L-\frac{g}{\lambda}\right)-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$
$$\varepsilon g=\alpha\lambda L-\alpha g-\rho\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (\alpha+\varepsilon)g=\alpha\lambda L-\rho$$
$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}$$

Scale effect

The formula shows the conclusion stated in the course: higher research productivity $\lambda$ raises growth, and a larger labour force $L$ raises growth when research is productive. If $\lambda=0$, increasing $L$ does not create this research-growth mechanism.

The model therefore predicts a scale effect: the larger the population size, the higher the growth rate. The course discusses this prediction at the country level and, when goods and patented ideas circulate, at the world level.

Main conclusions

Summing up

1

Economic growth comes from variety. Research raises the number of intermediate inputs $M_t$, and the production and GDP expressions show how this raises the quantities that grow with $M_t$.

2

Long-run growth is possible. The professor attributes this to the constant marginal return of the variety of intermediate input.

3

Openness to trade is good for growth. It can increase $M$ through trade and can increase R&D through the higher profits available to intermediate-good producers.

4

There is neither absolute nor conditional convergence. This is the conclusion stated for this model.

5

The decentralized economy is not optimal. The professor links this result to imperfect competition in the intermediate-good sector.

6

Tax policy can bring the decentralized economy to the optimal economy. The following policy section presents the two routes given in the course.

Openness to trade and growth: model prediction

3. Economic integration and trade

Consider the model with labour as the unique R&D input. The equilibrium growth rate of output established in the course is

$$g=\frac{\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}.$$

Now suppose two identical countries with fixed labour endowment $L=L^*$, where the star denotes the economy initially considered foreign.

a.

If the two countries merge and become a unique economy, what will be its growth rate of output?

Before integration, each country has labour endowment $L$ and the course gives $g=(\alpha\lambda L-\rho)/(\alpha+\varepsilon)$. After a merger, the unique economy has labour endowment $2L$.

$$\boxed{g_{\mathrm{merge}}=\frac{2\alpha\lambda L-\rho}{\alpha+\varepsilon}}$$

This applies the closed-economy result already established in the course to the combined labour force.

We will now look at what happens when, instead, trade is introduced between the two economies.

Suppose that, before trade starts,

$$M_0=M_0^*$$

(time zero denotes the moment trade starts).

Suppose, in addition, that the two economies produce, before trade, disjoint subsets of intermediate goods (specialization).

When trade is introduced, each monopolist can therefore sell its product in two markets.

Finally, assume that the production function of varieties depends only on the degree of domestic varieties.

b.

Compute the equilibrium profits and wages after trade is introduced.

In the closed economy, one variety produces $x$ for one market and earns $\Pi$. The statement says that, after trade, each monopolist sells its product in two markets. Its flow of profit is therefore the sum of the two identical market profits:

$$\boxed{\Pi^{\mathrm{trade}}=2\Pi}$$

The exercise also assumes that varieties used in final production depend only on domestic varieties. The supplied material does not provide enough information to reconstruct a different wage equation reliably; the wage formula already established remains $w_t=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}M_t$ for each domestic economy.

c.

Write down the research arbitrage condition after trade is introduced. Compare it to the one in the closed economy case. Discuss the result.

The closed-economy free-entry condition is $r=\lambda M_t\Pi/w_t$. With two-market profit $2\Pi$, the corresponding research-arbitrage condition is:

$$\boxed{r=\frac{\lambda M_t(2\Pi)}{w_t}}$$

Relative to the closed economy, trade changes the innovation value through the profit flow earned in two markets. This is the channel stated in the exercise and in the course summary: higher profits raise the incentive for R&D.

$$M_0<M_0^*.$$

Social Optimum in the model

5. Social optimum in the model

Consider the model from section 3.2.2. Now, instead of looking at the market equilibrium, we focus on the socially optimal allocation, from a social planner with one-period horizon.

The planner seeks to maximize

$$GDP_t=L_1^{1-\alpha}\int_0^{M_t}x_i^\alpha di-\int_0^{M_t}x_i.$$
a.

Solve for the social planner’s allocation of intermediate input. Compare it to the market equilibrium level. Explain your results.

With identical varieties, the planner’s objective can be written as $GDP_t=M_t[L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x]$. For a given $M_t$, the planner chooses $x$ to maximize the term in brackets.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(L_1^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-x\right)=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}-1=0$$
$$x^{1-\alpha}=\alpha L_1^{1-\alpha}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x^{\mathrm{SP}}=L_1\alpha^{1/(1-\alpha)}}$$

The market equilibrium was $x^{\mathrm{market}}=L_1\alpha^{2/(1-\alpha)}$. Since $0<\alpha<1$, the planner allocation is higher. This comparison uses the chapter’s imperfect-competition mechanism: the planner directly maximizes GDP, while the market quantity came from monopolistic firms maximizing their own profit.

b.

What is the social planner’s allocation of labor to R&D activities?

The supplied course material does not contain enough information to reconstruct this correction reliably. It gives a one-period planner objective over intermediate inputs, but does not provide the planner’s dynamic problem or the condition needed to choose $L_2$.

c.

Compare the efficient growth rate of the economy given by the social planner’s maximization problem to the market equilibrium growth rate. Interpret your findings.

The supplied material does not give enough equations to derive an efficient growth-rate formula. It does state the conclusion used for the comparison: the centralised economy achieves a higher growth rate than the decentralized economy, because the decentralized economy is not optimal under imperfect competition.

Economic policy

Economic Policy in an economy with variety of goods

Solving the centralised economy problem leads to a higher growth rate than the one achieved in the decentralized economy.

The professor therefore moves from the decentralized economy to policy. The objective is to reproduce the centralized outcome in a decentralized economy, because imperfect competition makes the decentralized allocation non-optimal.

1 · Subsidizing intermediate goods

Subsidize purchases of intermediate goods so that their effective price equals their production cost. The aim is to make the effective use of intermediate goods correspond to the production-cost allocation.

A lump-sum tax on households finances the subsidy without distorting their consumption-saving arbitrage.

2 · Subsidizing final goods

Subsidize final-good production so that demand for intermediate goods equals the demand that would prevail if their price equalled production cost.

This subsidy is also financed by a lump-sum tax on households.
