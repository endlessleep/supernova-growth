Chapter 4 — Economic Growth

Growth with Creation–Destruction

The Aghion–Howitt model

Introduction

Growth through creative destruction

Main idea

In the Aghion–Howitt model, firms compete to innovate. Better technology raises economic growth.

1

Create a better input

A successful innovation produces a new intermediate good: an input used by other firms.

2

Replace the old input

The new input makes production more efficient. It replaces the old technology, which becomes obsolete.

3

Earn a temporary reward

A patent gives the innovator monopoly profits. The next successful innovation can destroy these profits.

Why “creative destruction”?

The innovation creates a better technology and destroys the old producer’s position.

Aghion and Howitt.

Framework

Three sectors and one household

This simplified model has one intermediate good. Its structure is close to Romer’s model.

Final-good sector

Combines labour and the intermediate input. Firms face perfect competition and take prices as given.

Intermediate-good sector

Uses the final good to produce the input. In this one-input model, the current producer has monopoly power.

Research sector

Spends resources to improve technology. Success is uncertain. The research sector is perfectly competitive.

More research

Higher current spending raises the chance of success.

More advanced technology

For the same spending, a higher starting technology level makes further progress harder.

Household

Chooses consumption over time. Labour has no direct cost in its utility function.

Households and production

Households

Consumption, wealth and optimal growth

The household chooses consumption $c(t)$ over an infinite horizon:

$$\max_{\{c(t)\}}\int_0^\infty \frac{c(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$

The fraction measures utility from consumption. $\varepsilon$ controls its curvature. The factor $e^{-\rho t}$ gives less weight to future utility; $\rho$ measures impatience.

Labour and prices taken as given

Labour

$L$ is fixed. There is no population growth and no direct utility cost of work.

Wages and interest

The household takes $w(t)$ and $r(t)$ as given. We use a constant interest rate $r$; solving the model also gives a constant rate.

Income adds to wealth $S(t)$; consumption reduces it:

$$\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=r(t)S(t)+w(t)L(t)-c(t)$$

$r(t)S(t)$ is interest income and $w(t)L(t)$ is labour income. Consumption $c(t)$ is spending. The left side measures the change in wealth.

The transversality condition

Discounted wealth must approach zero in the long run:

$$\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)e^{-rt}=0$$
Optimal consumption growth
$$g_C=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

Control: consumption $c(t)$. State: wealth $S(t)$. The multiplier $q(t)$ is the shadow value of wealth.

1 · Current-value Hamiltonian
$$\mathcal H=\frac{c(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}+q(t)\,[rS(t)+w(t)L-c(t)]$$
2 · Consumption choice and wealth equation
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial c}=c(t)^{-\varepsilon}-q(t)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad q(t)=c(t)^{-\varepsilon}$$
$$\frac{dq(t)}{dt}=\rho q(t)-\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial S}=\rho q(t)-rq(t)$$
$$\frac{1}{q(t)}\frac{dq(t)}{dt}=\rho-r$$
3 · Differentiate the consumption condition
$$\frac{dq(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon c(t)^{-\varepsilon-1}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$
$$\frac{1}{q(t)}\frac{dq(t)}{dt}=\frac{-\varepsilon c(t)^{-\varepsilon-1}}{c(t)^{-\varepsilon}}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c(t)}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$
4 · Combine the conditions
$$-\varepsilon\frac{1}{c(t)}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=\rho-r$$
$$\boxed{g_C\equiv\frac{1}{c(t)}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}=\frac{r-\rho}{\varepsilon}}$$

Consumption growth depends on the gap between the return to saving $r$ and impatience $\rho$. The parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the size of this response.

Final good

The production function

Final-good firms combine labour and the intermediate input. The production function is:

$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$

Output and the intermediate input

$Y_t$: final-sector output. $x_t$: quantity of the intermediate input.

Technology and labour

$A_t$: technology. $L$: fixed labour. Together they form effective labour $A_tL$. The exponents are $1-\alpha$ and $\alpha$, with $0<\alpha<1$.

A higher $A_t$ means better technology and more efficient production.

Accounting

Intermediate-good production and GDP

One unit of input costs one unit of final good. Producing $x_t$ units therefore uses $x_t$ units of final output.

Subtract this input-production cost from final-sector output. With one input, $X_t=x_t$:

$$GDP_t=Y_t-X_t$$

The remaining output pays for consumption $C_t$ and research $R_t$:

$$GDP_t=C_t+R_t$$

Research uses the final good

There is no physical investment here. Research uses final goods, so it is included in GDP alongside consumption.

Perfect competition

The price of the intermediate input and wages

Competitive firms pay each input its marginal product: the extra output from one more unit, holding other inputs fixed.

The intermediate-input price
$$p_t=\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^{\alpha-1}$$

$p_t$ is the intermediate input’s marginal product. The final-good price is one, so $p_t$ is measured in units of final good.

Hold $A_t$ and $L$ fixed when differentiating.

1 · Competitive input demand
$$p_t=\frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial x_t}$$
$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$
2 · Differentiate with respect to the input
$$\frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial x_t}=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}\,\alpha x_t^{\alpha-1}$$
$$\boxed{p_t=\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^{\alpha-1}}$$
The wage
$$w_t=(1-\alpha)L^{-\alpha}A_t^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$

$w_t$ is labour’s marginal product: the payment for one unit of labour, measured in final goods.

Hold technology $A_t$ and the intermediate input $x_t$ fixed.

1 · Competitive labour demand
$$w_t=\frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial L}$$
$$Y_t=A_t^{1-\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$
2 · Differentiate with respect to labour
$$\frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial L}=A_t^{1-\alpha}(1-\alpha)L^{-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$
$$\boxed{w_t=(1-\alpha)L^{-\alpha}A_t^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha}$$
Monopoly profit

Profit maximisation in the intermediate-good sector

The producer chooses $x_t$ to maximise profit: revenue $p_tx_t$ minus cost $x_t$.

$$\Pi_t=p_tx_t-x_t=\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha-x_t$$

The price depends on how much the producer sells. It uses this demand relationship when choosing $x_t$.

Profit-maximising quantity
$$x_t=A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}$$

Choose $x_t>0$, holding $A_t$ and $L$ fixed, with $0<\alpha<1$.

1 · Substitute demand into profit
$$\Pi_t=p_t(x_t)x_t-x_t$$
$$\Pi_t=\left[\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^{\alpha-1}\right]x_t-x_t$$
$$\Pi_t=\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha-x_t$$
2 · First-order condition
$$\frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial x_t}=\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^{\alpha-1}-1=0$$
3 · Isolate the quantity
$$\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^{\alpha-1}=1$$
$$\frac{\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}}{x_t^{1-\alpha}}=1$$
$$x_t^{1-\alpha}=\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}$$
$$x_t=\left[\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$\boxed{x_t=A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}}$$
4 · Check that profit is maximised
$$\frac{\partial^2\Pi_t}{\partial x_t^2}=\alpha^2(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}(\alpha-1)x_t^{\alpha-2}<0$$

For a given $\alpha$, the chosen quantity depends on technology $A_t$ and market size $L$.

$$\Pi_t=\pi A_tL$$
$$\pi=(1-\alpha)\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$
1 · Evaluate profit at the chosen quantity
$$\Pi_t=\alpha(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}\left[A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right]^\alpha-A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$\Pi_t=A_tL\left[\alpha^{1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right]$$
2 · Rewrite the exponents
$$1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}=\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{2}{1-\alpha}&=\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1\\\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}&=\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1}\\&=\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\alpha\end{aligned}$$
3 · Factor the common term
$$\Pi_t=A_tL\left[\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\alpha\,\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right]$$
$$\Pi_t=A_tL(1-\alpha)\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$\boxed{\Pi_t=\pi A_tL},\qquad\pi\equiv(1-\alpha)\alpha^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$

$\Pi_t$ is total monopoly profit. The small $\pi$ is a fixed coefficient that depends only on $\alpha$. The term $A_tL$ captures technology and market size.

Production

Final-good output and GDP

Insert the producer’s chosen input quantity into production:

Final-good output
$$Y_t=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}A_tL$$
1 · Substitute the optimal intermediate quantity
$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$$
$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}\left[A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right]^\alpha$$
2 · Collect the powers
$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha}(A_tL)^\alpha\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$Y_t=(A_tL)^{1-\alpha+\alpha}\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$$
$$\boxed{Y_t=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}A_tL}$$
GDP after deducting intermediate production
$$GDP_t=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)A_tL$$
1 · Deduct the cost of intermediate production
$$GDP_t=Y_t-X_t,\qquad X_t=x_t$$
$$GDP_t=A_tL\left[\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right]$$
2 · Rewrite the exponent, then substitute
$$\frac{2}{1-\alpha}=\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}+2$$
$$\begin{aligned}\alpha^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}&=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}+2}\\&=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\alpha^2\end{aligned}$$
3 · Factor
$$GDP_t=A_tL\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)$$
$$\boxed{GDP_t=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)A_tL}$$

$Y_t$ is final-sector output. GDP deducts the final goods used to make the input; this gives the factor $1-\alpha^2$. Both expressions depend on $A_t$.

Research and innovation

Research

Success, failure and technological progress

Research can succeed or fail. Success improves technology; failure leaves it unchanged.

If research succeeds

$$A_t=\gamma A_{t-1},\qquad \gamma>1$$

Technology becomes $\gamma$ times its previous level. A larger $\gamma$ means a larger improvement.

If research fails

$$A_t=A_{t-1}$$

The economy keeps using the previous technology.

The success probability $\mu_t$ depends on research spending relative to the target technology level:

$$\mu_t=\lambda\left(\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}}\right)^\sigma,\qquad 0<\sigma<1$$

Efficiency

$\lambda$: how effective research is.

Research intensity

$R_t/(\gamma A_{t-1})$: spending relative to the target technology.

Response

$0<\sigma<1$: more spending raises the chance of success, but at a decreasing rate.

More spending: a higher chance of success. Higher starting technology: a lower chance for the same spending.

Research incentives

The researcher’s expected profit

A successful researcher becomes the producer of the new intermediate good and receives its monopoly profit.

The allocation rule in this simplified model

If research fails, the old input is assigned randomly to a producer. This producer may differ from the previous one.

With improved technology, monopoly profit is:

$$\Pi_t^*=\pi(\gamma A_{t-1})L$$

Research costs are paid even if the project fails. Expected profit is:

$$\mu_t\Pi_t^*-R_t=\lambda\left(\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}}\right)^\sigma\Pi_t^*-R_t$$
1 · The reward conditional on success
$$\Pi_t=\pi A_tL,\qquad A_t=\gamma A_{t-1}\ \text{if successful}$$
$$\Pi_t^*=\pi\gamma A_{t-1}L$$
2 · Weight the two outcomes
$$\mathbb E[\text{research profit}]=\mu_t(\Pi_t^*-R_t)+(1-\mu_t)(-R_t)$$
$$=\mu_t\Pi_t^*-\underbrace{[\mu_t+(1-\mu_t)]}_{1}R_t$$
$$=\lambda\left(\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}}\right)^\sigma\pi\gamma A_{t-1}L-R_t$$

The cost is certain; the reward is uncertain. More spending raises both the chance of success and the cost.

Research choice

Optimal spending and the probability of success

Maximising expected profit gives the research spending below, relative to the target technology:

$$\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}}=(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}$$

Hold technology and the parameters fixed. Assume $0<\sigma<1$ and positive $\lambda$, $L$ and $A_{t-1}$. This interior solution applies when the implied success probability is below one.

1 · Express the choice as research intensity
$$z\equiv\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}},\qquad R_t=\gamma A_{t-1}z$$
$$F(z)\equiv\mathbb E[\text{research profit}]$$
$$F(z)=\lambda z^\sigma\pi\gamma A_{t-1}L-\gamma A_{t-1}z$$
$$F(z)=\gamma A_{t-1}\left[\lambda\pi Lz^\sigma-z\right]$$
2 · First-order condition
$$F'(z)=\gamma A_{t-1}\left[\sigma\lambda\pi Lz^{\sigma-1}-1\right]=0$$
$$\sigma\lambda\pi Lz^{\sigma-1}=1$$
3 · Solve for research intensity
$$\frac{\sigma\lambda\pi L}{z^{1-\sigma}}=1$$
$$z^{1-\sigma}=\sigma\lambda\pi L$$
$$z=(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{R_t}{\gamma A_{t-1}}=(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}}$$
4 · Check the maximum
$$F''(z)=\gamma A_{t-1}\sigma\lambda\pi L(\sigma-1)z^{\sigma-2}<0$$

Research spending depends on profit $\pi$, market size $L$ and research efficiency $\lambda$.

Insert this spending choice into the success-probability formula:

$$\mu=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}$$
1 · Insert optimal research intensity
$$\mu=\lambda z^\sigma,\qquad z=(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}$$
$$\mu=\lambda\left[(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}\right]^\sigma$$
$$\mu=\lambda(\sigma\lambda\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}$$
2 · Collect the powers of λ
$$\mu=\lambda^{1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}$$
$$\begin{aligned}1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}&=\frac{1}{1-\sigma}\\\lambda^{1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}&=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}\end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mu=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}}$$

With fixed parameters and population, $\mu$ is constant. Keep the roles separate: $\mu$ is the chance of success; $\gamma$ is the size of success.

Externalities

Three effects of innovation beyond the researcher

Firms choose research to maximise their own expected profit. Three effects fall outside this private calculation:

1

A benefit larger than the monopoly rent

The innovator’s monopoly profit is smaller than the total benefit created for the economy.

2

A higher technological level for future research

Innovation raises $A$. In this model, that makes future success harder for a given research budget.

3

Destruction of the previous innovation

The new technology replaces the old one. The new producer gains, but the previous producer loses its monopoly profits.

Economic growth and competition

Growth

From uncertain innovation to expected growth

GDP and technology grow at the same rate because the other terms in GDP are fixed. There are two possible outcomes each period:

1 · Write GDP at both dates
$$GDP_t=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)LA_t$$
$$GDP_{t-1}=\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)LA_{t-1}$$
2 · Substitute into the growth rate
$$g_{GDP,t}=\frac{GDP_t-GDP_{t-1}}{GDP_{t-1}}$$
$$g_{GDP,t}=\frac{\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)L(A_t-A_{t-1})}{\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)LA_{t-1}}$$
3 · Cancel the identical factor above and below

The common factor is $\alpha^{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha^2)L$.

$$\boxed{g_{GDP,t}=\frac{A_t-A_{t-1}}{A_{t-1}}=g_{A,t}}$$
With probability $\mu$: innovation succeeds

Technology moves from $A_{t-1}$ to $\gamma A_{t-1}$. Its proportional increase is:

$$g_A=\frac{\gamma A_{t-1}-A_{t-1}}{A_{t-1}}=\gamma-1$$
With probability $1-\mu$: innovation fails

Technology remains at $A_{t-1}$, so $g_A=0$ for that period.

Expected growth combines the probability of innovation with the size of the productivity improvement:

$$E(g_A)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}(\gamma-1)$$
1 · The two possible growth rates
$$g_A=\begin{cases}\gamma-1&\text{with probability }\mu,\\0&\text{with probability }1-\mu.\end{cases}$$
2 · Take the expectation
$$E(g_A)=\mu(\gamma-1)+(1-\mu)\times0=\mu(\gamma-1)$$
3 · Substitute the success probability
$$\mu=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}$$
$$\boxed{g=E(g_A)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}(\gamma-1)}$$

Read the two ingredients separately

Expected growth = chance of success × size of improvement. Actual growth still depends on whether research succeeds in that period.

Determinants

What determines the economy’s growth rate?

$$g=E(g_A)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}(\sigma\pi L)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}(\gamma-1)$$

Three factors drive expected growth:

1

Research efficiency: $\lambda$

Better research methods increase the chance of success, so expected growth rises.

2

The size of innovation: $\gamma$

A larger innovation gives a bigger productivity gain when research succeeds. This is the term $\gamma-1$.

3

Population and market size: $L$

A larger market offers higher monopoly profits. Firms spend more on research, which raises expected growth.

Extension

Extension to non-drastic innovation

An innovation is non-drastic when competitors can still put pressure on the innovator. Imitation lowers its price and profit.

Lower profits → less research → lower growth. Too much competition can weaken the reward for innovation.

Policy implications

R&D policy

Why the two models give different policy answers

Private return

The benefit received by the firm that innovates.

Social return

The benefit to the whole economy, including effects on other firms.

1 · Variety of intermediate goods

Positive externality

A new variety raises the productivity of other firms. The innovator does not receive all these benefits.

1

Benefits spread to other firms

The economy gains more than the innovator earns.

2

Social return exceeds private return

The firm compares its research cost with its own profit. It leaves out benefits received by others.

3

Private R&D is too low

Firms do less research than is best for the economy.

Policy implication

Subsidise R&D to reduce its private cost and encourage more innovation.

A research project can be valuable for the economy even when the firm’s own expected gain is too small. The positive externality explains the gap between private and socially optimal R&D.

2 · Creative destruction

Negative effect · business stealing

A successful innovator replaces the previous monopolist and destroys its profits. The new firm does not include this loss in its decision.

Positive effect · wider benefits

Better technology benefits the economy. The innovator captures only part of this gain.

1

The new firm sees a profit opportunity

It counts the profit it expects to earn if it wins.

2

The previous firm loses its profit

The entrant does not subtract this loss when deciding how much to spend.

3

Private incentives can be too strong

Part of the private gain comes from replacing another firm. This can push R&D above the social optimum.

Dominant effectR&D compared with the social optimum
Positive externalityToo little research can result.
Negative externalityToo much research can result.
Result to remember

In creative destruction, R&D can be too low or too high. The answer depends on which effect dominates. An R&D subsidy is therefore not always justified.

R&D returns

Private gains and wider benefits

Private return

Studies suggest that private R&D returns are high, often above returns on physical investment.

Wider benefits

The total return also includes gains received by others. The size of the gap is difficult to measure.

Policy tools

Tax incentives and targeted subsidies

Both tools lower the private cost of R&D. The comparison here is broad tax support versus targeted funding.

Tax incentivesTargeted subsidies
How?Reduce the firm’s tax bill.Provide direct public funding.
Who?Firms across all sectors that meet the rules.Selected sectors, firms or projects.
SelectionA common eligibility rule.A choice of activities or recipients to support.
Remember the contrast

Tax incentives: broad access. Targeted subsidies: selected recipients. “All firms” means all firms that meet the eligibility rules.

Institutions

Match policies to the type of growth

Flexicurity · protect people, not jobs

Support workers while allowing firms, jobs and technologies to change. The aim is not to preserve every existing job.

Catching-up countriesCountries at the frontier
Main taskAdapt existing technology.Create new technology.
RiskMore limited.Research is risky.
FinanceLong-term bank–firm relationships.Venture capital.
Firm structureLarge firms can use economies of scale.Competition supports research and product markets.

Catching up

The main task is to adapt known technologies. Scale and stable bank funding can help.

At the frontier

The task is uncertain research. Venture capital is better suited to this risk. The government cannot reliably choose the future winner, so competition matters.
